Four games on injured reserve. A stat line that still leads Washington’s entire running back room. And a return date that just got confirmed.

That’s where the Washington Commanders’ offense stands heading into Monday night against the Cincinnati Bengals.

It’s been a few days since that big Thursday night win in New York, and the mood around this team is something I haven’t been able to say much this year: good.

There’s a lot to cover: the running back who’s coming back, a defense that keeps taking the football away, and where Washington now sits in the playoff picture.

Welcome to Commanders Floor. Hail to the Commanders. Let’s go.

Part One: Jacory Croskey-Merritt Is Coming Back

Let’s start with the offense.

Adam Peters said something on the radio that stuck with me as I started focusing on the Bengals matchup. He said running back Jacory “Bill” Croskey-Merritt is slated to return against Cincinnati. Another report said the same thing the day before.

So I think that pretty much confirms it: Croskey-Merritt will be back for the Bengals game.

And Washington has really missed him.

The Timeline

Croskey-Merritt was placed on injured reserve before the 49ers game. Since then, he’s missed four games: the 49ers, the Eagles, the Rams, and the Giants.

Four games is the minimum a player has to miss before he’s eligible to come off injured reserve. So the timing lines up exactly.

To put the injured-reserve rule in plain terms: a player placed on IR has to sit for a minimum number of games before he can be activated. In this case, the minimum was four. Croskey-Merritt has now served every one of them, which is why his return lines up with the Bengals game and not a week earlier.

A player coming back from that kind of absence may need a snap or two to find his rhythm. But he should be ready to carry a real share of the work.

Part Two: Why This Matters So Much

Why does it matter? Because this running game has been a real problem.

On paper, Washington’s rushing yards per game rank in the middle of the league. But in reality, the offense hasn’t been committing to the run, and when it does, the production hasn’t been there.

The Rest of the Room

Look at who’s been carrying the ball.

Rachaad White signed for about two million dollars, so nobody expected him to be a heavy producer in a lead-back role. He has 200 yards on 47 carries this season. Against the Rams, he led the team with just 28 rushing yards.

Austin Ekeler is coming off a big injury, so I didn’t expect him to be great either. He has 16 carries for 67 yards this year, and he’s been the number-two back and sometimes the number one.

They are what they are. And that’s just not going to cut it.

The rest of the depth chart has been hit hard too. Jeremy McNichols, who was doing pretty well, went on injured reserve and is likely lost for the season. Craig Reynolds is on the practice squad, and nobody was expecting him to come in and carry the load. Robert Henry Jr. was let go from the practice squad.

Croskey-Merritt Still Leads the Room

Here’s the wild part. Even after missing four straight games, Croskey-Merritt still leads Washington’s running backs in rushing yards this season.

He has 239 yards at 3.6 yards per carry, with two rushing touchdowns. He also has five catches for 40 yards, so he hasn’t been bad in the passing game either.

White, with 200 yards on 47 carries, is the next closest.

So, like it or not, Croskey-Merritt is still the number-one running back on this team, and that’s who Washington will ride for the rest of this year.

Part Three: Where I Stand on Croskey-Merritt

Let me be clear about where I stand.

I support the idea that Washington needs to go out and get a true number-one running back next year. I think Croskey-Merritt fits better as a number-two or number-three back. I just don’t think he’s built to be the long-term lead back.

He’s shown flashes. But ultimately, he’s a seventh-round pick for a reason.

But Right Now, He’s Massive

That said, the main point today is simple: Washington is getting a big weapon back for an offense that’s been struggling to put points on the board and sustain drives.

Think about what it would mean if he could take one to the house just occasionally. Not every drive. Not every game. Just once in a while.

I didn’t think I’d be saying “we’ve really missed him” after his first few weeks of the season, because he wasn’t great early on. But look at the alternatives. Getting Croskey-Merritt back is massive, especially if this team wants to make a run.

My Plan for the Backfield

I’m all for letting him come back, giving him a lot of the carries, committing to the run game, and using White as a change-of-pace back.

I’m tired of watching this run game every single week. Washington’s two backs combine for about twenty carries a game, while a team like the Bills gives James Cook close to thirty carries some weeks. Washington doesn’t have a James Cook, so there’s a difference. But when Croskey-Merritt isn’t on the field, the gap in commitment is huge.

Part Four: Why One More Back Changes the Math

There’s a simple way to think about why one more back matters for an offense that’s struggling.

When a team has no running threat, defenses can sit back and wait for the pass, and the quarterback has to do everything himself.

When the ground game produces a few explosive plays, defenses have to respect it, and the passing game opens up.

That’s why even an occasional long run changes the math for a unit that can’t sustain drives.

The Jayden Connection

This connects directly to what we’ve been talking about with Jayden Daniels.

Before the Giants game, Jayden had been as passive as he’d been at almost any point in his career: 70 percent of his throws against the Rams went behind the line of scrimmage or within nine yards. One reason is that defenses haven’t had to respect Washington’s run game. They’ve been sitting back in two-high safety looks, taking away the deep ball, and daring Washington to run.

Against the Giants, we saw what happens when Jayden gets aggressive. He threw three touchdown passes and led his first game-winning drive in almost two years.

Now imagine that aggressive Jayden with a running game that forces safeties to creep up. That’s when the deep shots open up. That’s when play-action actually works. And that’s when this offense starts to look like 2024 again.

Croskey-Merritt isn’t a superstar. But he might be exactly the piece that makes the rest of the offense easier.

Part Five: The Defense Keeps Taking the Ball Away

Now let me turn to the defense, because Washington’s defense keeps forcing turnovers. You might have thought it was a fluke. Look at the numbers, and it isn’t.

Washington has a turnover margin of plus-seven. That’s better than some of the best teams in the NFL. A team with that kind of margin would normally be closer to seven-and-two than three-five-and-one. It’s an elite turnover ratio.

And it happens seemingly every game. During the Giants game, when Frankie Luvu forced that fumble, I was literally waiting for it, because Washington gets a takeaway at some point in every game.

The Jump From Last Year

Here are the numbers that tell the story.

In 2025, through seventeen games, Washington had ten takeaways.

In 2026, through nine games, Washington already has fourteen.

It didn’t start well this year, but boy, has it heated up. Since Week 3, Washington has forced a turnover in every single game.

Look at that jump on its own: ten takeaways in an entire season last year, and fourteen in nine games this year. That isn’t a small improvement. It’s a change in how this defense plays. And because it’s held up for so many straight games, I’m treating it as a pattern, not luck.

Why Takeaways Matter So Much for This Team

A takeaway does two things at once. It ends the opponent’s drive, and it gives your offense a short field.

For a team whose offense struggles to sustain drives, that’s a lifeline. Every turnover turns a long, hard march into a much easier one.

So the defense is quietly doing a lot of the offense’s work. And when the offense does capitalize, like it didn’t after Nick Cross’s interception against the Rams, the margin for error grows a lot.

Part Six: The Peanut Punch Culture

Daronte Jones has his players flying around. I’ve never seen so many players throwing the “peanut punch” at the football. Even on special teams, a player did it against the Giants to force a fumble.

You saw signs of it in Week 1 against the Eagles. I said at the time that it was the most physical I’d seen this team play against Philly. Players were flying around.

Why the Peanut Punch Matters

A ball carrier protects the football with his arm. But defenders who are taught to attack the ball on every tackle will eventually knock it loose.

That’s a habit, not a trick. It shows up on the stat sheet as a steady stream of forced fumbles. And it only works if the whole defense plays that way.

That’s the kind of culture a defensive coordinator builds over time.

Part Seven: The Full Defensive Picture

Let’s look at the full defensive numbers.

Against the run: Washington still hasn’t allowed a 100-yard rusher this season. That includes Christian McCaffrey and Jonathan Taylor.

I know it can feel like the run defense has slipped lately. Blake Corum had a good game for the Rams, and Cam Skattebo had a good game for the Giants on Thursday. But Washington still held them to reasonable totals, and the run defense ranks among the better units in the league in yards allowed per carry.

Against the pass:

2,341 net passing yards allowed.
Opponents have completed 217 of 332 passes, 65.4 percent.
Six interceptions.
Seven yards per pass attempt allowed.
18 team sacks.
17 passing touchdowns allowed.

Overall: Washington has allowed 26 total touchdowns, nine rushing and seventeen passing, with zero defensive or special-teams touchdowns allowed. Opponents are 14 of 19 on field goals.

What the Numbers Say

The pass defense still has work to do. Seventeen passing touchdowns allowed is too many, and we saw it again on Thursday with Isaiah Likely’s 66-yard touchdown.

But the first thing to notice about this defense is the turnover rate. You can count on a takeaway every single game.

Part Eight: The Future of This Defense

When you put these pieces together, and you give Daronte Jones a true free safety and a few more pieces, I really think this defense could start to look like the Brian Flores defenses in Minnesota a few years from now.

Not this year. But once Jones gets a few of his own coaches here to fully teach his scheme, and starts developing these players, it could become something special.

And maybe keep Steve Wilks, too. He’s been doing a nice job, and the players seem to love him. Since Wilks moved to the sideline for game days, Washington has gone two-and-one.

Keep a few of your guys, keep working with the scheme, keep bringing the blitzes, and this could turn into something. This looks like a physical, fast defense with an identity.

Peters’s Free-Agency Class Deserves Credit

I also said Adam Peters had a good free-agency class, and with all the criticism of Peters lately, he deserves credit for this part.

He brought in Nick Cross, who’s been praised at nickel and intercepted Matthew Stafford against the Rams.

He brought in Amik Robertson on a two-year, sixteen-million-dollar deal. A lot of us asked what Robertson was doing here, and he’s had a strong season, leading the league in pass breakups for a stretch.

He brought in Odafe Oweh, who now has five sacks. It hasn’t been a perfect year for Oweh, but he’s been productive.

Those are new faces, a new identity, and a new physicality for this defense.

So yes, we’ve criticized Peters on this channel for the Tunsil trade, the center situation, and the quiet trade deadline. But the defensive free-agency class is a big reason this defense is forcing turnovers every week. Fair is fair.

Part Nine: The Playoff Picture

Now let’s talk about where Washington stands after the Giants win.

Washington is three-five-and-one. The Giants are five-three-and-one. That’s a two-game gap, with Washington holding a head-to-head win after the earlier tie.

The rest of the NFC East has been struggling. Dallas has been inconsistent, and the Eagles have been in free fall without Jalen Hurts.

This is still a division nobody owns. And for the first time in weeks, Washington looks like a team with some momentum.

The Bengals Game Matters

Monday night, November 23rd, against the Bengals is the next step.

Here’s what Washington needs:

One: commit to the run with Croskey-Merritt. Give him a real workload, not ten carries.

Two: let Jayden attack early. The aggressive Jayden we saw at the end of the Giants game needs to show up in the first quarter, not just the final two minutes.

Three: keep the turnover streak alive. One takeaway per game is the floor. Two would be even better.

Four: tighten up the pass defense. No more 66-yard touchdowns to tight ends.

Five: protect Jayden. Nick Allegretti started at center against the Giants, and Washington won. Keep building that chemistry.

Part Ten: Why the Mood Is Different

I want to end on the mood, because it really is different this week.

For most of this season, every week felt like a crisis: injuries, end-of-game mistakes, the Quinn hot seat, the trade deadline.

This week feels different. Washington won a must-win game on the road. Jayden delivered his first game-winning drive in almost two years. Stefon Diggs, the player Washington kept at the deadline, scored the winning touchdown. And now the team’s leading rusher is coming back.

That doesn’t fix everything. The record is still three-five-and-one. The offense still has a lot to prove. And the coaching questions haven’t gone away.

But for the first time in a while, there’s a real reason for hope.

Final Word From Commanders Floor

Let me put it all together.

The running back boost: Jacory Croskey-Merritt is slated to return against the Bengals after missing four games on injured reserve. Even with the missed time, he still leads Washington’s running backs with 239 yards at 3.6 yards per carry and two touchdowns.
