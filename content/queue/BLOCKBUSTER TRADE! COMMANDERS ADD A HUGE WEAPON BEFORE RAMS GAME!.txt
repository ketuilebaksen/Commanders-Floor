Trey Amos was targeted five times against the Eagles, and I couldn’t find a single catch he allowed that went for a first down. That’s where I want to start, because after a win that came with plenty of caveats, the individual performances are what stand out.

Washington finally got back in the win column on Sunday night, scoring 27 unanswered points against Philadelphia. I came away with a clear sense of who deserves credit, and those names matter more than ever, because with the trade deadline four days away, Adam Peters is deciding who’s part of the future and who’s for sale.

And there’s real trade news, too. Ian Rapoport says Washington could land a 25-year-old receiver for a third- or fourth-round pick. More on that in a minute.

Welcome to Commanders Floor. Hail to the Commanders. Let’s go.

Part One: The Context, Because It Matters

First, let’s be honest about the game.

This was a home game, in prime time, coming off a bye, against a banged-up opponent with a backup quarterback, on a short week after a Monday night road game. Washington needed to win this one.

It also started slowly. Philly jumped out to a 10–0 lead, and the penalty problems the team said it would fix during the bye didn’t really get fixed.

So, honestly? I didn’t learn a ton about this team as a whole. That doesn’t mean the performance wasn’t good, or that there weren’t strong individual efforts. I’d just be careful about reading too much into a game with that many favorable circumstances.

How I Grade Players

A win flatters everyone, and a loss punishes everyone. So I try to separate individual performance from the scoreboard. A player can have a great game in a loss, or a poor game in a win.

The question I ask is simple: did this player make his team more likely to win, and by how much?

Through that lens, several players had strong games. Four names stood out most to me, and I’m going to hand out my own game ball at the end.

Part Two: Trey Amos. Welcome Back.

My first standout is Trey Amos, and welcome back, because you were deeply missed.

Amos’s first game back was the 49ers game, when he played a little over 55 percent of the snaps. Against the Eagles, he was fully back, playing roughly 88 percent.

In base looks, Rasul Douglas was the one who came off the field. In sub packages, Amos and Douglas played outside, with another player sliding inside to nickel. Dan Quinn also praised Nick Cross’s work at nickel this week.

My view of Amos hasn’t changed since the offseason. He’s a starting NFL corner. As a rookie, he played seven or eight games on a poor defense before injuries hit, and he stood out as a bright spot. Against the Eagles, he stood out again.

What He Did on the Field

On the All-22, I didn’t see Amos give up a single meaningful catch. On one play, his man caught the ball for about seven yards, and Amos pushed him out of bounds short of the first-down marker.

He had two pass deflections. On one of them, an Eagles receiver actually beat him off the line, but Amos had the size and speed to close the gap and get a hand in, preventing a big play.

He also had an interception wiped out by a roughing-the-passer penalty, and he nearly had another on a quick slant later in the game.

Now, the context: he played a team missing several offensive linemen, its starting quarterback, and some of its top receivers. So I’m tempering expectations. I’m not calling him an All-Pro. But you felt his impact.

Why Amos Matters More Than It Looks

Think about what Amos’s return means for this defense.

With a legitimate corner on each side, the coordinator can play more man coverage, take more chances up front, and trust the back end to hold. When a defense doesn’t have that, it has to hedge: drop extra defenders, rush fewer players, and give up pressure. The pass rush and the secondary work as one unit. A stronger secondary makes the rush more dangerous.

And the numbers back that up. In Weeks 1 through 5, before Amos returned, Washington ranked 25th in defensive EPA against the pass and 27th in success rate against the pass. In the two games with him, Weeks 6 and 8, it ranked seventh and fifth.

Correlation doesn’t prove causation, but the tape agrees. The defense looks more secure.

With Amos, Amik Robertson, and Douglas, Washington has a legitimate NFL cornerback room. Not the league’s best, but far from the worst. I’m comfortable with it now, though I still need to see Amos against better competition, and that test comes Sunday against the Rams.

Part Three: Treylon Burks. I Owe Him Credit.

My second standout is Treylon Burks, and I owe him some credit.

I never doubted his ability, only his health. Over his first four seasons, he played in 11, 11, 5, and 8 games. You can’t count on someone that unavailable.

Credit to him, though. He’s stayed healthy this year and has been even better than I imagined, consistently making big plays.

Against the Eagles, he caught only three passes, but they went for 81 yards, which led the team. That’s the kind of production you expect from a number-one receiver, even on a small number of targets.

For a guy who’s six-foot-two and 225 pounds, he can really run. The best example was a 54-yard play built on a classic shot concept: two go routes outside with a post over the middle. Burks sold an outside move and then ran right past Quinyon Mitchell, a first-team All-Pro corner.

He had two plays of 20-plus yards.

The flaw: he dropped one of the easiest throws he’ll ever get, a third-and-three spot route with nobody near him. He has a drop problem, but he balances it with real big-play ability.

Burks’s Role Going Forward

With Terry McLaurin out for the season, Burks is a starting receiver the rest of the way. There’s no conversation to be had.

He’s 26, and I hope Washington re-signs him, though he’ll have a market the way he’s playing.

One more number: in yards per route run, Burks is tied with McLaurin at 1.74, tied for 36th among players with at least twenty targets. That’s starter-level efficiency.

Part Four: Antonio Williams. Always Open.

My third standout is Antonio Williams, and I couldn’t overlook him.

Remember, he did this against a defense run by Vic Fangio, featuring two first-team All-Pro corners. This kid is always open. It’s like driving through a small town and seeing a motel with an old-school “Vacancy” sign in the window. You start to wonder if it’s ever sold out. Williams is open for business at all times.

Even on Burks’s 54-yard play, Williams was down at the bottom of the screen, beating his defender on a go route.

People talk about his toughness, his catches in traffic, and his route running. Nobody talks enough about his straight-line speed. Back in the Colts game, he ran away from the defense on an 87-yard play that was wiped out by a penalty, through no fault of his own.

He led the team with five catches on seven targets for 54 yards. That’s modest. But what struck me was a stretch in the first half when Jayden Daniels kept looking for him on must-have downs. Diggs is on this roster, and Jayden still said, “Let me find Williams.” Jayden wasn’t accurate early and missed him on at least one throw, but the focus was clear.

Last week I said that if Williams got fewer than seven targets against Philly, David Blough was getting it wrong. He got exactly seven. Blough passed the test, barely.

Why Williams Is the Real Deal

What I love about Williams is how his skills fit together. His route running creates separation, his hands finish the play, and his toughness lets him win in traffic. A lot of receivers have one or two of those traits. Few have all three.

When a young player combines them, he becomes a quarterback’s security blanket on third down. That’s exactly what Jayden was reaching for in the first half.

Adam Peters said the sky’s the limit for Williams. If he grows into what he looks capable of, this could become a perennial 1,200-to-1,300-yard receiver. If I’m Blough or Dan Quinn, I make Williams a focal point the rest of the way.

Watching Burks, Williams, and Chig Okonkwo grow into bigger roles is fun, even though I hate the reason it’s happening.

Part Five: Shy Tuttle and the Defensive Line

My fourth standout is Shy Tuttle, and the context matters here too.

Daron Payne and Javon Kinlaw didn’t play. The Eagles had a backup offensive line, a hobbled Saquon Barkley, and a backup quarterback. But Washington’s defensive line was its most consistent unit, especially against the run.

Tuttle stepped in alongside Tim Settle, and Daronte Jones changed the approach, using more four-down fronts instead of the five-down looks Washington had been using.

Tuttle played 32 snaps and finished with six tackles, three run stops, and a sack. On the All-22, he controlled his gaps, stayed disciplined, and anchored against double teams, which let the linebackers and safeties get downhill.

People asked whether it was a coincidence that the line had its best game with Payne and Kinlaw out. It isn’t that simple. The Eagles had injuries, a backup quarterback, and missing receivers, and Jones changed the structure into a look Philly hadn’t scouted. Don’t be reductionist about it.

Part Six: The Commanders Floor Game Ball

Now the game ball, and this is my pick.

All four had strong nights. But the player who changed the most about how Washington plays is Trey Amos.

Burks made the biggest plays. Williams was the most reliable target. Tuttle anchored the run defense. But Amos is the reason Washington’s pass defense went from 25th and 27th to seventh and fifth in the two games he’s played. He’s the reason Daronte Jones can be more aggressive. He’s the reason the defense finally looks like it trusts its back end.

Trey Amos, Commanders Floor game ball. Now go do it against the Rams.

Part Seven: The Trade News. Josh Downs.

Now let’s get to the trade news, because the market is moving fast.

Josh Downs is the name to watch.

Downs is a 25-year-old receiver for the Indianapolis Colts. Last week, he requested a trade as the Colts headed into a full fire sale. They’ve already traded Quenton Nelson and DeForest Buckner, and Jonathan Taylor is on the block too. Downs still played on Sunday, and he caught a touchdown.

On Monday, NFL insider Jordan Schultz told Ryan Clark on The Pivot that the Colts wanted a third-round pick for Downs, and he named Washington one of three teams to keep an eye on.

Then on Thursday, Ian Rapoport of NFL Network reported that Washington could land Downs for a third- or fourth-round pick. That means the asking price may be dropping.

It also sounds like this one won’t happen before Sunday. The reporting suggests Washington will have to wait until next week to submit a final offer, which makes sense, because the Rams game will tell Washington what kind of team it is.

There’s another angle: Washington might be interested in signing Downs to a long-term extension if it acquires him. That’s exactly the kind of buy we’ve been talking about. He’s young, he’d stick around, and he isn’t a rental.

Downs or BTJ?

Remember the bidding war we talked about earlier this week? The Steelers reportedly love Downs. The Chiefs reportedly see Downs as their backup plan if they can’t land Brian Thomas Jr.

So now Washington has two young receivers in play: Downs from the Colts and Brian Thomas Jr. from the Jaguars.

CBS Sports’s Zachary Pereles even proposed a BTJ deal built around Shy Tuttle plus a draft pick going to Jacksonville. Interesting timing, right after Tuttle’s best game of the season. Jacksonville would get draft capital and a defensive lineman who could help its 2026 push.

So which one do I want?

BTJ is the higher-ceiling player. He’s a former Pro Bowler with seventeen college touchdowns from Jayden, and he’s under contract through 2027 with a 2028 option.

Downs is likely the cheaper, safer path, at a third or a fourth, and he’s been productive in Indianapolis.

My answer: go hard after BTJ first. If the price climbs too high or Jacksonville won’t deal, Downs at a third or fourth is an excellent fallback. Either way, Washington walks out of the deadline with a young receiver for Jayden. What it can’t do is walk out with neither.

Part Eight: The Rams Twist. Dyami Brown?

Here’s a wild one. ESPN’s Aaron Schatz floated a trade sending Dyami Brown to the Los Angeles Rams, Washington’s opponent on Sunday, for a Day 3 pick.

His reasoning: Brown is on a one-year deal. He’d give the Rams a veteran field-stretcher while Davante Adams is out with a hamstring injury, and he’s known as a strong run blocker, which Sean McVay loves in his receivers.

Here’s my take. With Burks and Williams emerging, Brown’s role is shrinking. If the Rams or anyone else offers a Day 3 pick, that’s fine value for a one-year player. Just maybe not before Sunday’s game. Let’s not hand them a receiver right before we play them.

Part Nine: The Injury Concerns

Before we get to the Rams, a quick injury check, because it affects the deadline too.

Daron Payne is dealing with his foot injury. Javon Kinlaw and Johnny Newton have availability questions. That leaves the defensive front short, which is why Tuttle’s performance mattered so much.

And at running back, Jeremy McNichols has a midfoot injury that’s waiting on more scans, with Jacory Croskey-Merritt already on injured reserve. That could create another need, which is why the Jonathan Taylor conversation hasn’t completely gone away.

Part Ten: The Rams Game Decides It All

Sunday against the Rams is the last game before the November 10th deadline, and everyone agrees it could determine Washington’s final strategy.

Win, and Washington is three-four-and-one, two games out in a division where Dallas is three-and-five and Philly is two-and-six. Peters, who has talked to all 31 teams and sounds like a buyer, gets the green light to add a young receiver.

Lose, and Washington is two-five-and-one. The focus shifts to selling one-year players like Chaisson and Brown for Day 3 picks.

Here’s what I want to see on Sunday:

One: Trey Amos against better receivers. The Rams are a step up from a broken Eagles offense. This is his real test.

Two: Williams as the focal point. Seven targets again, minimum.

Three: Burks catching the easy ones. The big plays are there. Now clean up the drops.

Four: the defensive line without Payne and Kinlaw. Can Tuttle and Settle do it again against a better offensive line?

Five: a fast start. Going down 10–0 worked against Philly. It won’t work against the Rams.

Final Word From Commanders Floor

Let me wrap it up.

From the Eagles win: Trey Amos gets the Commanders Floor game ball for changing how this defense plays. Treylon Burks led the team in receiving yards with big-play speed. Antonio Williams was always open and got seven targets. Shy Tuttle anchored the run defense with six tackles, three run stops, and a sack.

But the context matters: Philly was banged up, had a backup quarterback, and was on a short week. Don’t read too much into it.

The trade news: no deal is done. But Rapoport says Washington could land Josh Downs for a third- or fourth-round pick, and a final offer may come next week. Brian Thomas Jr. is still in play too. My plan is BTJ first, Downs as a strong fallback.

And Sunday against the Rams decides whether Peters buys or sells.
