Six-time All-Pro. One sack allowed all of last season. And the trade-deadline clock is ticking.

ESPN’s Jeremy Fowler says the Indianapolis Colts are expected to be open for business, and there’s a guard sitting in that building who could be exactly the shield Jayden Daniels has been begging for. In a minute, I’ll tell you who he is and exactly what Adam Peters would have to pay.

Then there’s Bill Barnwell’s idea of shipping a veteran lineman out of Ashburn for a sixth-round pick, and why that lineman isn’t the one to blame. And finally, a fan poll that gives Washington thirteen percent odds to win the NFC East. Thirteen percent. I’m sorry, but that’s disrespectful.

Welcome to Commanders Floor. Hail to the Commanders. Let’s go.

Part One: How This Started, a Long Way From Ashburn

Let’s take this step by step, because it started in Nashville.

On Sunday, the Colts lost 26–20 to the Titans, a Titans team that walked in without a single win. Then Indianapolis fired Chris Ballard, its general manager for almost ten years. Assistant GM Ed Dodds takes over as interim for the rest of the season.

Two-and-four, GM gone, interim in charge. We all know how that movie usually ends.

Jeremy Fowler of ESPN said rival teams expect the Colts to be open for business at the deadline. He also said it’ll be fascinating to see whether they put Quenton Nelson on the market, since Nelson is in the final year of his contract. Jordan Reid, also of ESPN, posted that he’s interested to see Nelson’s value if Indy makes him available: thirty years old, on an expiring deal.

Now the honest part, because this channel doesn’t sell smoke.

No insider has said Washington is talking to Indianapolis. Not one. What we have is a roundtable column that dropped this morning calling our offensive line this team’s biggest liability, and pitching a run at Nelson for a second-round pick or a combination of picks. And we have this channel screaming for it.

One more thing: the Colts don’t have to sell. Owner Carlie Irsay-Gordon said she expects the team to keep competing to win football games. Maybe that’s just Monday talk, or maybe she means it. Keep both possibilities in mind.

Part Two: Who Quenton Nelson Is

If you’re new to this, here’s who we’re talking about.

He was the sixth overall pick in the 2018 draft. He has made the Pro Bowl in every one of his eight seasons, the first player in Colts history to do that. He’s a six-time All-Pro: three first-team selections from 2018 through 2020, plus three second-team nods, the latest one this past January.

In his entire career, he has missed four games. Four, in eight seasons, at one of the most violent positions in football.

And here’s the number that made me write this script. In 2025, according to PFF, Nelson finished fourth among every guard in the league with an 84.5 overall grade. He played 637 pass-blocking snaps and allowed nineteen pressures. That’s a pressure rate under three percent. He allowed one sack all season, in Week 14 against Jacksonville.

Nineteen pressures in a whole year, bro. Remember that number, because now I’m going to show you ours.

Part Three: What We’ve Got Right Now

On Monday night in San Francisco, Jayden Daniels got put on the ground four times. We ran for 53 yards. We converted three of twelve third downs.

According to Sports Info Solutions data cited by David Harrison, Nick Allegretti, Brandon Coleman, Josh Conerly, and Lucas Patrick all rank among the forty worst players in the NFL in blown-block rate on running plays. Coleman also ranks among the twenty worst in pass protection.

Now, Coleman moved to left tackle because Laremy Tunsil hasn’t played since tearing his triceps in training camp. That’s not easy, and I respect him for taking it on. But according to Athlon, Coleman has already allowed 22 pressures and four sacks in six games.

Put that next to Nelson. Coleman: 22 pressures in six games. Nelson: 19 pressures in seventeen.

And the roundtable column says Chris Paul is struggling at left guard as well.

Part Four: Why the Middle Matters Most for Jayden

This is the tactical part, and it’s the part I care about most.

Outside pressure doesn’t kill Jayden Daniels. He can dodge it. He’s a magician escaping the edge. He breaks contain, gets out of the pocket, and makes a play out of nothing.

What kills him is pressure up the middle. When the interior collapses, Jayden has nowhere to step up and nowhere to escape. The ball comes out rushed, or it doesn’t come out at all.

Look at the sacks on Monday, and look at the intentional grounding at the end. The pocket closed on him from the front, not from the side.

A guard like Nelson fixes exactly that. He gives you solid ground in the middle, right where Jayden stands to read the defense.

And in the run game, in gap and duo schemes, Nelson is a tractor. He wins leverage, drives the defensive tackle two yards back, and opens running lanes for our backs.

Fifty-three rushing yards on Monday. I don’t need to say anything else.

Part Five: The Price and the Money

The roundtable column says a second-round pick. The trade simulator used by A to Z Sports also values Nelson at a 2027 second.

Nelson makes eighteen million dollars in 2026, sixteen million of it base salary. Because the team that acquires him only pays what’s left of the season, A to Z figures a trade before the deadline would clear about eight million dollars for the Colts. Washington would be the team covering that eight million.

Do we have room? Spotrac’s table has Washington among the teams with the most cap space in the league. That number moves with every transaction, so I won’t swear to an exact figure. But cap space isn’t the problem. The problem is the will.

And Adam Peters has already shown us that when he wants a guy, he goes and gets him. He went and got Laremy Tunsil from Houston. He went and got Deebo Samuel. This general manager isn’t scared of the phone.

Part Six: How He Fits

Nelson walks right in at left guard, next to Coleman, and that cleans up Jayden’s entire blind side.

Coleman is learning to play tackle in the middle of the season. With Nelson beside him, he stops worrying about what’s happening to his right, because the guy next to him doesn’t lose one-on-ones.

Chris Paul goes back to competing for a spot or becomes a high-end backup. All of a sudden, this line has real depth.

There’s also something that doesn’t show up on the stat sheet: interior twists and stunts. Those are the crosses where the defensive tackle and the edge rusher trade lanes, and you beat them with communication. Nelson has spent eight years reading those crosses and calling them out to his center.

According to Next Gen Stats, through Week 5, Washington used nine different offensive-line combinations, one of the highest totals in the league. A line that keeps changing like that hasn’t had time to learn how to talk to itself. A veteran like Nelson teaches the rest of them in the huddle what no coach can teach in a week of practice.

Part Seven: The Arguments Against, Answered One by One

There are some arguments against this, and I’m going to take them seriously.

One: he’s thirty. He’s a guard, not a running back. Elite guards play well into their thirties, and this one has missed four games in eight years.

Two: he’s a rental. If you don’t extend him, he walks in March. That’s the trick, though. You’re not just buying nine games. You’re buying an exclusive window to negotiate an extension before he hits free agency. He’s in your locker room, and you get to convince him to stay. If you do, the anchor of the interior line for Jayden’s prime years is already in the building.

Three: we’re one-four-and-one. Why buy with that record? Hold that thought until the last part of this video, because that’s where I’ll show you why the record is lying.

Four: a guard doesn’t fix your left tackle. Whoever says that is right. Nelson doesn’t fix Coleman. But if the interior holds, Jayden can do what he does best: climb the pocket and break out to whichever side he wants. With a solid interior, a shaky tackle is a problem. With a broken interior, not even the best tackle in the world saves you.

Part Eight: My Stance

I give up the second-round pick today.

And I’ll go further. I know this is going to light up the comments.

If Peters has to choose between spending picks on a receiver to replace Terry McLaurin or spending them on Nelson, I’m taking Nelson, and I’m not thinking twice.

A receiver does you no good if your quarterback is lying in the grass. On Monday, the problem wasn’t who was catching the ball. It was that Jayden didn’t have time to throw it.

And yes, I know what we talked about this week: Terry’s season-ending injury, the Tyreek Hill conversation, the idea of moving Daron Payne for a veteran receiver. Those debates are real. But if I can only fix one thing before November 10th, I fix the pocket. Everything else in this offense depends on it.

Look, I could be wrong. If the Colts ask for a first-round pick, I hang up the phone. A first for a thirty-year-old guard on an expiring contract is too much. And if Indianapolis decides not to sell, this whole conversation is over.

But if the price is a second and Peters doesn’t even call, that’s when I’m going to be mad.

Tell me in the comments, and don’t give me “it depends.” A second-round pick for Quenton Nelson: yes or no?

Part Nine: If Somebody Walks In, Somebody Walks Out

If an All-Pro guard walks in, somebody has to walk out of the picture, and ESPN already picked who.

Let me be clear from the first sentence: nobody has cut or traded Andrew Wylie. This is a proposal.

Bill Barnwell published his ESPN column with eleven trades that make sense before the deadline, and one of them involves Washington. In his proposal, the Patriots get Andrew Wylie and a 2027 seventh-round pick, and Washington gets a 2027 sixth-round pick. In other words, we move up a rung in the draft in exchange for a veteran who isn’t playing.

Why the Patriots? Because in their game against the Jets, they lost Alijah Vera-Tucker, Morgan Moses, and Ben Brown, and were left with five healthy offensive linemen. Five. They need bodies now.

Barnwell’s case for Wylie is his versatility. He can play right tackle or either guard spot, so he can fill in across the line without much trouble.

So far, so good. But the next part of the column is what stuck with me. Barnwell points out that Wylie is buried on Washington’s depth chart and has played just fifteen snaps this season, even with Tunsil out.

Fifteen snaps. Think about what that says about us.

Our starting left tackle is out. Coleman had to switch positions. We’ve used nine different line combinations. Four of our linemen are among the worst in the league at blowing run blocks.

Meanwhile the veteran who can play three spots, a guy with 93 regular-season starts who didn’t allow a single sack in the 2024 playoffs, has played fifteen snaps.

There are two possible explanations, and both are bad.

Explanation one: the staff watches Wylie every day in practice and sees that he’s done. This year’s numbers, in a tiny sample, point that way. PFF has him at a 33.8 overall grade and 4.6 in pass protection. Yes, it’s only fifteen snaps, I know. But 4.6.

Explanation two: Wylie can still help, and the staff is wrong not to use him. If that’s the case, somebody please explain how a line taking on this much water chooses not to give snaps to the most versatile veteran it has.

Both can’t be true at the same time. And either way, somebody got it wrong, and it isn’t Wylie.

It’s on Peters.

Part Ten: The Roster-Building Mistake

This is the stance that’s going to cost me a few angry comments.

Adam Peters re-signed Wylie in February on a two-year deal. Spotrac has him at a 3.2-million-dollar cap hit this year. According to Athlon, he counts 3.875 million next year, with only 425 thousand dollars in dead money if you move him.

Divide this year’s cap hit by the snaps he’s played, and it comes out to more than two hundred thousand dollars per snap. I know that’s cheating a little, because the season isn’t over, but it still hurts.

Peters built this line believing the veterans were the insurance: Wylie, Allegretti, Patrick. And when Tunsil went down, which is exactly when you cash in your insurance, the insurance never took the field.

That’s a roster-building mistake. It’s a small one, but it’s a mistake. And I’m saying that as someone who has defended Peters on almost everything he’s done since he got here.

My stance: sell Wylie for the sixth right now. Athlon nailed the headline when it compared a sixth for a guy who doesn’t play to finding money in your jeans pocket. You save almost three and a half million dollars on the 2027 cap, and you move up a rung in the draft.

But, and this is key, Wylie only leaves if somebody comes in. Barnwell suggests Washington could use one of its practice-squad tackles as the swing tackle. If you sell Wylie and bring nobody in, you get even thinner up front.

That’s why these two stories are really one move. Wylie out, Nelson in. A sixth comes in, a second goes out, and the line gets better in the middle. That’s what I’d do if I were Adam Peters.

And if you tell me Wylie is the depth guy and it’s dangerous to be without him, I’ll answer you with one number: fifteen snaps. We’re already playing without him.

Tell me in the comments: does Wylie go for a sixth, or is the real problem a staff that doesn’t know how to use him?

Part Eleven: Thirteen Percent? Really?

Before you type “why buy if we’re one-four-and-one,” I’ve got a number for you.

Pro Football Rumors ran a poll asking who wins the NFC East. 412 people voted. Giants, 42 percent. Dallas, 33. Philadelphia, 13. Washington, 13.

Thirteen percent, tied with the Eagles, dead last.

I’m not going to lie to you. There are reasons for that number. We’re one-four-and-one and last in the division. According to the NFL, we’ve converted 25 of 81 third downs, 30.9 percent, which puts us 31st in the entire league. And our defense lets opponents convert 44.4 percent of their third downs.

If you showed me those two numbers and nothing else, I’d vote thirteen percent too.

But now look at the rest.

We’re two games behind the Giants, with more than half the season left. Eleven games to play. The division leader has three wins. Nobody in this division is running away with anything.

The Eagles come to Landover on November 1st without Jalen Hurts, coming off a stretch of losses. We have the bye to get healthier and to install the tweaks Mike Garafolo reported are coming. We just added Steve Wilks to help with the defensive game plan.

And look at what actually killed us. The third-down failures come from getting into third-and-long, and that comes from second-down penalties and an offensive line that keeps getting pushed back into Jayden’s face. Fix the middle of that line, and the whole chain gets better: more manageable third downs, more time for Jayden, more points.

That’s why I’m not afraid to buy at one-four-and-one. The record says we’re a bad team. The details say we’re a team with a fixable problem in a division nobody owns. A Nelson trade doesn’t buy you a rental. It buys you a real shot at the most broken division in football, and a cornerstone for Jayden’s next five years if you extend him.

Thirteen percent? I’ll take that disrespect and save it for January.
