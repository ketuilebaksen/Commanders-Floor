Part One: Why There's No Trade

Let's get the facts straight first.

One: the trade deadline has passed. After November 10th, NFL teams can't trade players until the new league year begins in March.

Two: Sweat is under contract. He's an active Arizona Cardinal, signed through the 2028 season. He signed a four-year deal with Arizona in 2025 worth about 76 million dollars, or roughly 19 million per year.

Three: Arizona hasn't shown interest in moving him. According to ESPN's Josh Weinfuss, Cardinals general manager Monti Ossenfort said a few months ago that the team had no interest in trading Sweat.

Four: there's no report of Washington pursuing him. None. Every trade scenario you've seen this week is a hypothetical.

So if a video tells you Sweat is coming to Washington before Sunday, it's selling you a fantasy.

Part Two: Who Josh Sweat Is

Now, let's talk about the player, because he's worth talking about.

Sweat spent his first seven seasons in Philadelphia, from 2018 to 2024. In 2024, he led the Eagles with eight sacks in 16 games, and then he was a big part of the Eagles' Super Bowl run.

Then he signed with Arizona in 2025, and he had the best season of his career.

His 2025 Season

According to the source I worked from, in 2025 Sweat:

started all 17 games,
recorded 12 sacks,
forced four fumbles,
added 17 quarterback hits,
and had 13 tackles for loss.

The 12 sacks and four forced fumbles were both career highs. He was named a Pro Bowl alternate. And according to the Cardinals' official game notes, his strip-sack total led the NFL at one point that season.

He was the first Cardinals player to reach double-digit sacks in a season since J.J. Watt in 2022.

That's real, high-level production.

His 2026 Season So Far

But 2026 has been a different story.

According to ESPN's Josh Weinfuss, Sweat was a surprise addition to Arizona's physically-unable-to-perform list with a knee issue before the season.

And according to Pro Football Focus, as of late October, Sweat had a 58.2 overall grade, which ranked 85th out of 113 qualified edge defenders. He had 12 total pressures and two sacks on 243 snaps.

So the version of Sweat Washington sees on Sunday may not be the 12-sack player from 2025. He's been slowed this year.

That doesn't mean Washington can ignore him. Pass rushers with his track record can wreck a game at any time. But it's important context.

Part Three: Sweat Against Washington on Sunday

So what does Sweat mean for Washington's offense on Sunday?

The Left Tackle Matchup

Remember Washington's offensive line situation. Laremy Tunsil is still recovering from a torn triceps. Brandon Coleman has been learning left tackle on the fly all season.

Sweat lines up on both sides, but how Coleman and the right tackle handle him will matter.

The Center Question

And remember the center problem. Against the Bengals, Lucas Patrick played every snap at center, and Dan Quinn admitted his snaps were inconsistent in location and speed.

When the snap is off, Jayden Daniels loses a half-second. And a half-second is exactly what an edge rusher like Sweat needs to get home.

The Strip-Sack Danger

Here's the scariest part of Sweat's game: the strip-sack. Four forced fumbles in 2025. He doesn't just sack the quarterback, he goes for the ball.

Washington's defense has forced a turnover in every game since Week 3, and the offense has protected the ball well, with a zero turnover margin against Cincinnati. Jayden's ball security in the pocket will be critical on Sunday.

How Washington Can Handle Him

Here's how Washington can neutralize Sweat:

One: get the ball out quickly. David Blough's quick-game concepts were effective against the Giants' blitz-heavy defense.

Two: chip him. Use a tight end or running back to give the tackle help.

Three: move the pocket. Roll Jayden away from Sweat's side and let him attack on the run.

Four: run at him. Make an aggressive pass rusher defend the run. With Jacory Croskey-Merritt back, Washington has a real early-down runner again.

Five: fix the snaps. If the snap is clean, Jayden can read the defense instead of chasing the ball.

Part Four: The Hypothetical. Should Washington Want Sweat?

Now let's take the hypothetical seriously. Forget the deadline for a moment. If Sweat ever became available, in the offseason for example, should Washington be interested?

So Washington isn't empty at edge rusher. This isn't a matter of counting names and declaring the room empty.

The Real Question

The real question is whether another proven edge rusher would raise the ceiling of a group that already has multiple players at the position.

That distinction matters. An addition shouldn't be treated as an automatic upgrade just because his résumé looks impressive. The useful question is what Washington would gain that it doesn't already have, and whether that gain would be worth the cost.

Part Five: The Case For

Production. Twelve sacks in 2025 is the kind of number that changes a defense. Washington's pass rush has improved, but adding a player who's proven he can reach double digits would give Daronte Jones another weapon.

Disruption beyond sacks. Four forced fumbles and 17 quarterback hits in 2025. That suggests his value isn't only about finishing sacks. He creates turnovers, and Washington's defense has built its identity on takeaways this season.

Availability. Sweat started all 17 games in 2025, the second time in his career he started every game in a season.

Familiarity with the NFC East. He spent seven seasons in Philadelphia and knows the division well.

Part Six: The Case Against

The contract. Sweat is signed through 2028, with a cap hit of more than 20 million dollars in each of the next two seasons, and he's scheduled to become a free agent in 2029. That's a significant commitment for a player who'll be over 30 by then.

The 2026 decline. A knee issue, a stint on the PUP list, and a 58.2 PFF grade so far this season. One great year in 2025 doesn't guarantee the same output in 2026 or beyond.

The roster fit. Washington already has four outside linebackers on its active roster, including Oweh on a 100-million-dollar deal. Adding Sweat would mean spending heavy resources at a position where the team already has multiple options.

The cost. Any trade would require draft picks. And Adam Peters has been disciplined about not paying desperation prices, as we saw with the Aiyuk situation, which ended with Washington getting a former All-Pro for almost nothing.

Arizona's stance. The Cardinals have said they're not interested in trading him.

Part Seven: Tactical Fit

Let's say, hypothetically, Sweat were in Washington. How would Daronte Jones use him?

The Rush Decision

Picture a third-and-long. Washington has to decide whether Sweat should attack straight upfield or stay disciplined on the edge.

Send him hard at the quarterback, and the payoff is obvious: if he wins, the pocket collapses before the passer gets comfortable.

But there's a tradeoff. If the quarterback steps up or rolls away from the rush, Sweat may run himself past the play, turning pressure into a clean escape route. The rest of the front would have to close that space.

Keep his rush more controlled, and he can threaten the outside while protecting the edge, forcing the quarterback to decide whether to stay in a shrinking pocket or throw under pressure. That limits scrambles, but it gives the passer an extra beat.

Coordination With the Other Rushers

With Armstrong, Oweh, Chaisson, and Josephs already on the roster, Washington would have to coordinate assignments carefully: which edge rusher attacks, and which one is responsible for keeping the quarterback from escaping outside.

Two rushers charging without regard for the quarterback's path could create exactly the escape lane the defense wants to eliminate.

When Offenses Adjust

Now flip it. Suppose an opponent starts giving its tackle help against Sweat, with a tight end or running back chipping him.

Washington has a decision: keep sending Sweat at that reinforced side, or use the attention he draws to create pressure elsewhere.

If he stays there, the offense has to spend extra blockers on him, which can open up one-on-ones for Oweh or the interior rushers.

That's the real value of a pass rusher like Sweat: even when he doesn't get home, he changes how the offense protects.

Part Eight: The Same Lesson, Applied to Sunday

And here's the key point. Everything I just said about using Sweat applies in reverse to stopping him on Sunday.

Washington can chip him, roll the pocket away from him, and use quick throws. If Arizona keeps sending him hard upfield, Washington can make him pay with Jayden's legs, because aggressive edge rushers can open escape lanes.

Jayden is one of the best scrambling quarterbacks in the league. If Sweat over-pursues, Jayden can step up and take off.
