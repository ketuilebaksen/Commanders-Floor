Twenty-two to twenty.

Sixty-four yards in thirty-four seconds. Two deep shots from Jayden Daniels to rookie Antonio Williams. And Drew Stevens's 26-yard field goal as time expired.

The Washington Commanders just stunned Joe Burrow and the Cincinnati Bengals on Monday Night Football.

And on the same day, the San Francisco 49ers released Brandon Aiyuk. Ian Rapoport is already pointing him toward the DMV, and Jayden Daniels has already made peace with him.

Before you fall for any clickbait claiming "confirmed trade" or "Aiyuk signs with Washington," slow down. That hasn't happened, and I'll explain exactly why and when it could.

There's also a team with a Shanahan connection that could steal him from Washington. And a fourth-down number that should still worry everyone.

Welcome to Commanders Floor. Hail to the Commanders. Let's go.

Part One: The Win Nobody Expected

Let's start with the game, because nobody gave Washington a chance.

All ten NFL.com analysts picked the Bengals. One predicted a 63–21 blowout. Vegas had Cincinnati favored by around five and a half points. Burrow came in as the MVP favorite, and the Bengals had scored at least 31 points in five straight games.

Washington held them to 20.

How It Unfolded

According to ESPN, the first quarter was scoreless. The Bengals led 10–7 at halftime and were still ahead going into the fourth quarter.

Washington clawed back, and with 46 seconds left it led 19–17.

Then, according to the Bengals' official site, Evan McPherson drilled a 56-yard field goal with 46 seconds remaining to put Cincinnati ahead 20–19.

Northwest Stadium went quiet. It looked like another heartbreaker.

The Drive

Then Jayden went to work.

According to the AP recap on ESPN, Jayden connected with rookie Antonio Williams on two long pass plays, moving the Commanders 64 yards in 34 seconds.

That set up rookie kicker Drew Stevens for a 26-yard field goal as time expired.

Washington 22, Cincinnati 20.

The Antonio Williams Moment

Remember what we said all week. Against the Giants, Williams played 86 percent of the snaps but got just two targets. Dan Quinn said on Wednesday that he wanted the ball in Williams's hands more, and not only on third down.

And with the game on the line, with 34 seconds to go and Burrow on the other sideline, Jayden went to Williams. Twice.

That's not a rookie being featured. That's a rookie being trusted.

The Drew Stevens Moment

And remember what Quinn said on Friday about his rookie kicker: he gave Stevens long field goal attempts right from Week 1, he likes how he's developing, and he saw him stay out in the rain after a game to keep working.

On Monday night, Stevens was the one who sent Northwest Stadium home happy.

Holding Burrow

According to the Bengals' official recap, Burrow went 28 of 40 for 300 yards and two touchdowns. Those are good numbers, but not 31-point numbers.

Quinn's plan was disguise and complementary football. Washington didn't try to win a shootout. It kept the game close, kept Burrow under his recent pace, and gave Jayden the ball with a chance at the end.

That's exactly how Quinn said Washington had to win this game.

The Concern

It wasn't perfect. According to the Bengals' official site, edge rusher Boye Mafe had three sacks. The protection still has work to do.

But wins over 7–2 teams don't have to be perfect.

Even Ja'Marr Chase admitted it after the game, saying simply that Washington beat them.

What the Win Means

Washington is now 4–5–1, with two straight wins over the Giants and the Bengals. The Bengals fall to 7–3.

And it flips everything about the Aiyuk story. Before the game, the source I worked from said this: win, and signing Aiyuk is a playoff bet; lose, and it's an audition for 2027.

Washington won. So now it's a playoff bet.

Part Two: The 49ers Release Aiyuk

The soap opera is over. For weeks on this channel, it's been: will they cut him, won't they; does Jayden want him, does Jayden not want him; the posts about Jayden's mom.

On Monday, it happened. Adam Schefter reported, citing ESPN sources, that the 49ers were releasing Brandon Aiyuk.

At almost the same time, Ian Rapoport of NFL Network wrote the part that matters for Washington: all eyes now shift to the Commanders, who could offer Aiyuk a spot on their practice squad. Rapoport added that Washington is his most likely landing spot, especially now that he and Jayden are on the same page.

Rapoport doesn't throw that out there for nothing.

Why There's No "Trade"

Now, about the headlines claiming a "confirmed trade."

The trade deadline passed on November 10th. San Francisco couldn't trade Aiyuk to anyone. The 49ers had two options: release him, or start paying him.

They had a roster exemption that didn't cost them a dime. They stretched it to the last day, and then they let him go.

The Waiver Process

Schefter laid out what happens next. Aiyuk is subject to waivers. If and when he clears, he becomes an unrestricted free agent.

That means the other 31 teams can claim him first, with priority going to teams with the worst records.

On the league's standard schedule, that waiver window closes Tuesday at 4 p.m. Eastern.

But there's a catch: whoever claims Aiyuk on waivers takes on his San Francisco contract. That's why beat reporters expect nobody to claim him.

If nobody does, by Tuesday afternoon Aiyuk is a free agent who can sign wherever he wants.

So Washington couldn't sign him Monday. The earliest is Tuesday afternoon.

Should Washington Claim Him on Waivers?

Would I claim him on waivers just to lock it down? No. Even I'm not that crazy.

According to Spotrac, the contract runs to roughly 120 million dollars through 2028. Being aggressive is one thing. Lighting money on fire is another.

You wait until he clears, and then you make your offer.

Part Three: The Relationship Is Fixed

The reason half the DMV didn't want to hear Aiyuk's name had nothing to do with football. It was what he posted this summer about Jayden and about Jayden's mom. That made me mad too.

But on November 18th, Jeremy Fowler of ESPN reported that Aiyuk reached out to Jayden about a month ago to fix things, and that Jayden was receptive and understanding.

And on Monday, Ben Standig of The Athletic added another source confirming they made peace. Standig was careful to say that doesn't mean Jayden is recruiting Aiyuk. Credit to him for being clear about that.

Still, the biggest obstacle is gone.

What Rosenhaus Said

Pay attention to what agent Drew Rosenhaus said when asked what kind of team Aiyuk is looking for.

He described a team with a former coach, maybe a former teammate, someone who knows him: a former college teammate at Arizona State, someone in the front office who knows him.

Jayden played with Aiyuk at Arizona State. And Adam Peters was in San Francisco's front office when the 49ers drafted Aiyuk in the first round in 2020.

Rosenhaus didn't say "Washington." He described it.

Part Four: Practice Squad or Active Roster?

This is where the debate gets interesting.

Nicki Jhabvala of The Athletic wrote Monday morning that there are just as many compelling reasons for Washington to sign Aiyuk as not. And she ran the math on the practice-squad route.

Sign him to the practice squad, elevate him for game days under the elevation rules, and the cost for the rest of the regular season is very low. And if it doesn't work out, she noted, Washington could easily move on without losing much.

Rapoport mentioned the practice squad too. Low money, low risk.

Where I Get Off the Train

And this is exactly where I get off the train.

If Washington signs Aiyuk to the practice squad, Peters is playing not to lose instead of playing to win. Here's why.

One: practice-squad players aren't fully yours. Under NFL rules, any other team can sign a practice-squad player to its active roster at any time. You can protect a few practice-squad players each week, but those spots are limited, and you'd be burning one every week on a player who isn't playing.

Do you want a contender watching Aiyuk practice with Washington for two weeks and then scooping him up for its 53-man roster? Not me.

Two: the message. You're telling a former second-team All-Pro with college chemistry with your franchise quarterback that you don't trust him.

Aiyuk is coming off two years of fighting with everyone. What works with a player like that is someone saying, "I want you here right now." Not, "Come practice with the backups and we'll see."

Three: the calendar. Washington just beat the Bengals and is 4–5–1. There's no time for a month-long audition. If Aiyuk is healthy, and Rosenhaus swears he's 100 percent, let him practice for a week and then play.

Part Five: Why Washington Needs Him

Look at Washington's receiver room.

Terry McLaurin: tore his patellar tendon at San Francisco and is out for the season.
Dyami Brown: on injured reserve.
Another young receiver: on injured reserve.
Stefon Diggs: at 32, the number one, with 36 catches, 386 yards, and six touchdowns before Monday.
Antonio Williams: a growing rookie, and now a Monday Night Football hero.
Treylon Burks: a space player managing a foot issue.
Malik Heath: landed on the practice squad last week.

With all respect, that's thin for a playoff push.

What Aiyuk Was at His Best

Aiyuk at his best, in 2023, had 75 catches, 1,342 yards, and seven touchdowns, and earned second-team All-Pro.

He wins at the top of his routes with footwork, which is exactly what Washington is missing when defenses roll everything toward Diggs.

The On-Field Fit

Think about it on the field.

Diggs plays a lot in the slot and in the red zone, where his body control and timing beat almost anyone.

Aiyuk is different. He's an outside receiver who beats you on a 15-yard dig against Cover 3, or on a comeback tight to the sideline when the cornerback bails.

Those two profiles don't step on each other. One on each side, and the defense can't send safety help the same way every snap.

That changes Jayden's reads. Right now, when the defense rotates toward Diggs, the backside is Burks or Williams one-on-one.

With Aiyuk over there, the backside becomes the defense's problem. And with Williams emerging after Monday night, Washington could suddenly have three real options.

Part Six: Washington Isn't Alone

Be careful, though. Washington isn't the only option.

Seattle's offensive coordinator is Brian Fleury, who came from Kyle Shanahan's staff in San Francisco.

Think about that Rosenhaus description: someone who knows him. That describes Seattle too.

That's why the practice-squad route looks even worse to me. If this becomes a bidding war, whoever offers the 53-man roster and real snaps wins. Whoever offers "come practice and we'll see" loses.

Part Seven: The Part That Could Make Me Look Stupid

Now, the risks.

Aiyuk hasn't played since October 20th, 2024, when he tore his ACL, MCL, and meniscus on the same play.

Then came the mess with San Francisco. He left the team. The league says he owes the 49ers about 13 million dollars in bonuses. And Kyle Shanahan said Aiyuk's reinstatement meant absolutely nothing to his team.

James Jones said on NFL Network last week that he doesn't think we'll see Aiyuk play this year.

If Jones is right, this take is going to look embarrassing, and you'll remind me until the draft. I'll take it. I'd rather be wrong for being aggressive than right for being scared.

And to everyone who says they'll never forgive what Aiyuk said about Jayden's mom: if Jayden forgave him, what are we even talking about?

Part Eight: The Fourth-Down Problem

Now, signing Aiyuk doesn't help much if this team can't finish drives. And there's one number that explains the problem.

Thirty-eight percent. That was Washington's fourth-down conversion rate this season on 13 attempts, going into Monday night.

According to Commanders.com, in 2024, the NFC Championship Game season, Washington converted 87 percent. In 2025, it was 62 percent.

Eighty-seven. Sixty-two. Thirty-eight. Three years, one direction.

When Fourth Down Was the Identity

In 2024, fourth down was Washington's identity. Quinn showed up in the DMV and taught this team to play without fear. Fourth-and-2 at midfield? Washington went. Fourth-and-1 in the red zone? Washington went.

Defenses knew Washington wasn't punting, and that changed how they defended third down.

The Numbers Now

Going into Monday night, Washington ranked 29th in red-zone scoring attempts per game, 19th in points per play, and 30th on third down, at 33 percent.

You miss on third down, you miss on fourth, the drive dies. And the defense walks back onto the field tired.

David Blough's Quote

David Blough said last week that the team wants to take care of the football and keep finding ways to end drives with points.

Look at the order: take care of the ball first, points second. To me, that sentence explains the 38 percent better than any stat.

The Fan Criticism

People in the DMV are hammering Blough. They say he's predictable, that he has no ideas in the red zone, that they miss Cliff Kingsbury.

There's something to it. Washington's fourth-down calls this season have used less pre-snap motion and rarely force the defense to reveal its coverage.

But going for it or not on fourth down is the head coach's call. And the conversion rate depends on when you go. You convert far more often on fourth-and-1 than on a desperate fourth-and-6.

Thirty-eight percent on 13 tries suggests Washington has been going at the worst distances, often when already trailing. In 2024, Quinn went for it when it helped him. This year, he's been going when he has no choice.

Monday Night's Lesson

And on Monday night, Washington scored 22 points, with nine of them in the fourth quarter. Field goals were a big part of it. Against Burrow, settling for three can be dangerous, and it almost cost Washington.

But this time, Stevens and the defense made it work. The question is whether Washington can win that way every week.

Part Nine: The NFC East Picture

Sunday made Washington's life harder, and Monday made it easier.

According to the source, on Sunday the Giants beat Jacksonville 23–8 at MetLife, and the Jaguars came in at 7–2. The Giants are now 6–3–1 and lead the NFC East.

Dallas scored 51 points against Tennessee and improved to 5–6. And the Eagles beat Pittsburgh 19–6 and sit at 4–7.

Before Monday, Dean Jones of Riggo's Rag called the Bengals game a must-win. If Washington had lost, it would've been 3–6–1, three games behind the Giants with seven to play.

After the Win

Instead, Washington is 4–5–1, two games behind the Giants, with seven games left. And remember, Washington is 1–0–1 against the Giants this season, which matters for tiebreakers.

The remaining schedule: at Arizona, at Tennessee, home against Houston, home against Atlanta, at Minnesota, at Jacksonville, and home against Dallas.

It's still an uphill climb. But it's a real race now.

Part Ten: Dan Quinn's Job

The source I worked from had a strong take before the game: if Washington lost to Cincinnati, Peters should fire Dan Quinn the next day.

Riggo's Rag wrote that Quinn is fighting for his job, and CBS had him sixth on its coaching hot-seat list last week.

A home loss in primetime, with the Giants pulling away, would've been the worst possible night for Quinn.

Instead

Instead, Quinn got exactly the kind of win he talked about all week: complementary football, disguise on defense, a quarterback who took his shots in the clutch, a rookie receiver trusted when it mattered, and a rookie kicker who delivered.

Two straight wins, over the Giants and the Bengals.

I'm not saying the job security questions are gone. The fourth-down numbers are still a problem, and the record is still under .500.

But Quinn can breathe this week.
