Washington scored just three points over the final 44 minutes against the Rams.

Let that sink in. Washington led 10–6 at halftime, and then the offense basically disappeared. And on the other side of the ball, a defensive line missing its two best interior players couldn’t create the kind of game-changing pressure that wins close games.

So this week, a lot of fans have been asking the same question: what if Washington added a real interior pass rusher? And one name keeps coming up in those conversations: Detroit Lions defensive tackle Alim McNeill, who already has a career-high five and a half sacks this season.

Before we go any further, let me be crystal clear. This is a hypothetical, not a reported negotiation. And with the trade deadline already passed, it can’t happen this season. But the McNeill conversation tells us exactly what Washington needs, and the good news is that Washington already has two real boosts on the defensive line heading into Thursday night.

Part One: The Frustration Isn’t Just the Score

For Commanders fans, the frustration right now isn’t simply that Sunday ended in a 20–13 loss.

It’s that the game gave us a clear picture of how quickly a promising start can turn into another afternoon where the offense can’t find the scoreboard.

Washington led 10–6 at halftime. Then it produced just one field goal the rest of the game.

The defense did its part. Nick Cross intercepted Matthew Stafford in the third quarter, the perfect chance to put the game away. And Washington gained just nine yards on the next four plays and settled for three points.

That’s the kind of sequence that makes this team’s problems impossible to pin on one position group. Washington needs its offense and defense to complement each other, and against Los Angeles, the pieces didn’t connect often enough.

From Hope to Pressure, Again

The win over Philadelphia showed what a better result looks like. But the loss to the Rams immediately cranked the pressure back up, with the Giants coming on Thursday night.

The Giants lead the division. Washington is heading into that game after another loss, with very little time to reset.

That makes every personnel conversation feel urgent. But it doesn’t make every proposed move automatically smart.

Part Two: The Defensive Line Situation

Let’s start with the defensive line, because that’s where this conversation begins.

Washington’s interior has been gutted. Daron Payne hurt his foot in practice on October 30th, reportedly a season-ending injury. Javon Kinlaw went on injured reserve the same day with a knee injury. Dan Quinn has said both are likely out for the season.

That’s roughly 44 million dollars of cap space on injured reserve, and Washington’s two best interior defenders off the field.

Boost Number One: Johnny Newton Is Back

Here’s the first real boost: Washington activated Johnny Newton on November 7th, after his recovery from a chest injury.

That’s meaningful. Newton, the second-round pick who was supposed to be part of this defensive line’s future, is described in the team’s own summary as crucial to a depleted defensive front.

Remember what Quinn said when Newton’s 21-day practice window opened: the arrow is going up, and he’s very optimistic. Now Newton is back on the active roster.

The interior was already the biggest point of concern on this defense, and getting a key young player back can help a lot.

Boost Number Two: Ricky Barber Earned His Spot

The second boost came a few days earlier. On November 4th, Washington promoted Ricky Barber from the practice squad to the 53-man roster.

He’s the undrafted defensive lineman Washington cut and brought back, the guy who sacked Dak Prescott in his NFL debut on Christmas Day 2025, and the guy who led the team in tackles with a sack in the preseason opener against Miami. Dan Quinn has said he likes him on the edge and as a pass rusher.

So the interior rotation now looks like this: Shy Tuttle, who had six tackles, three run stops, and a sack against the Eagles but was limited this week with a shoulder injury; Tim Settle; Johnny Newton, just activated; and Ricky Barber, newly promoted. Veteran Daniel Ekuale is on the practice squad for depth.

That’s two real additions in one week, at the position where Washington needed them most.

Part Three: But One Activation Doesn’t Solve Everything

Let’s stay realistic, though.

One activation doesn’t erase the question of how much pressure this group can generate over a full game, especially when Washington needs more chances to change the rhythm of an opponent’s offense.

The Rams game showed why pressure matters even when it doesn’t solve everything. Washington intercepted Stafford, but Los Angeles still found a way to score the decisive touchdown early in the fourth quarter.

Washington had chances to make the game swing, and the Rams still left with the win.

A more disruptive interior presence could, in theory, create more difficult downs and give the defense another path to those momentum-changing plays. That’s the football argument.

But a defensive tackle can’t be a shortcut around Washington’s bigger problems.

The Offense Is the Bigger Issue

Remember: Washington scored three points in the final 44 minutes. An interior pass rusher can affect what the defense does. He can’t directly fix late-game scoring.

We’ve covered the offensive problems all week: an offensive line that couldn’t handle Myles Garrett in the second half, a running game where Rachaad White led the team with just 28 yards, Jayden Daniels throwing 70 percent of his passes no more than nine yards downfield against the Rams, and end-of-game mistakes, including a delay of game on fourth down and a checkdown with nine seconds left.

No defensive tackle fixes any of that.

Part Four: Why Alim McNeill’s Name Came Up

So where does Alim McNeill fit into this?

McNeill is a defensive tackle for the Detroit Lions, and his production this season is the strongest part of the hypothetical.

Through eight games in 2026, he has five and a half sacks, already a career high, along with six tackles for loss and eleven quarterback hits.

Those numbers describe a player who is affecting the backfield in more than one way.

For Washington, the appeal is obvious. If that kind of interior disruption carried over, it would give the defensive front another way to pressure quarterbacks and make opposing offenses work harder to sustain drives.

The Three Ways to Think About the Fit

First, as a pass rusher. McNeill’s five and a half sacks and eleven quarterback hits show he’s been generating pressure from the interior. With Washington’s line depleted, a healthy player producing those numbers is an intriguing idea.

Second, his effect on everyone else. Interior pressure, in principle, makes it harder for a quarterback to step up into the pocket, and gives edge rushers like Odafe Oweh and K’Lavon Chaisson more chances to finish plays. That’s general football logic, not a promise about how it would work in Washington’s specific scheme.

Third, the timing. Washington is coming off a loss with a short week against the division leader. A move made under pressure can feel satisfying because it signals action. But a new player wouldn’t erase the need for the whole team to work together, which is exactly the point Dan Quinn emphasized after the Rams game when he talked about complementary football.

Part Five: Why It Won’t Happen This Season

Now for the biggest obstacles, and there are several.

Obstacle one: the trade deadline has passed. The deadline was Tuesday, November 10th, at 4 p.m. Eastern. Washington made no moves. Players under contract with other teams can’t be traded again until after the season. So a McNeill trade is impossible this year.

Obstacle two: he’s not available. McNeill isn’t an unsigned player waiting for an opportunity. He’s an active, productive player for Detroit, on a four-year extension that represents a serious financial commitment. There has been no report that Washington has interest, that Detroit is willing to move him, or that any talks have taken place.

Obstacle three: the injury history. McNeill tore his ACL in December 2024, which ended that season and affected his availability and performance in 2025. He’s healthy now and played in Detroit’s November 8th game. That matters, but so does the history.

Obstacle four: sustainability. His 2026 numbers are clearly better than what his career totals through 2025 would suggest. That rebound makes him interesting, but it also means the numbers need context. Any team would have to decide whether it’s paying for lasting impact or reacting to one strong stretch. And reports describe his run defense as uneven from game to game.

So this isn’t a trade rumor. It’s a fit exercise. And now it’s a fit exercise for the offseason, if anything.

Part Six: Why Washington Stood Pat, and Why It Matters Here

Let’s connect this to the deadline, because it’s directly related.

Washington stood pat on Tuesday. According to the reporting, the Commanders believe they’re still in the NFC East race, and Adam Peters needs to win games. So the front office kept Stefon Diggs, kept K’Lavon Chaisson, kept its expiring veterans, and didn’t add from outside.

That means the defensive line answer for the rest of 2026 has to come from inside the building: Newton, Barber, Tuttle, and Settle.

That’s why Newton’s activation matters so much. He’s essentially Washington’s “trade deadline acquisition” on the defensive line. Not a new player, but a returning one, at the exact position where Washington needed help.

Part Seven: The Tactical Side. How Interior Pressure Works.

Let’s get into the football, because this applies to Newton and Barber just as much as it would to McNeill.

Situation one: third-and-long.

What happens when Washington gets to third-and-long, and the quarterback has one clear escape route: stepping up through the middle of the pocket?

That’s the first tactical choice. Washington can ask its interior defender to attack the middle instead of just occupying space and letting the quarterback get comfortable.

It sounds simple. It isn’t.

If the interior rusher pushes into the middle, the quarterback has less room to hitch, reset, and throw downfield. A sack is one possible payoff. But even pressure that forces an early throw can change the play. The offense might have to settle for a short completion, throw the ball away, or throw while moving off its spot. For a defense trying to get a stop, that’s the difference between a drive continuing and a punt.

The Offense Gets a Choice Too

The offense isn’t just going to stand there. It can:

Keep an extra blocker in.
Slide the protection toward the interior rusher.
Have the quarterback get rid of the ball quickly, before the rush arrives.

Each answer changes the picture. Keep more blockers in, and there are fewer receivers running routes. Get the ball out fast, and the defense has to tackle cleanly so a short gain doesn’t become a first down.

That’s why pressure isn’t just about one player reaching the quarterback. It’s about the choices the offense has to make before the snap, and the timing it has afterward.

If the quarterback sees an interior threat and speeds up his process, the defense may have changed the play without recording a sack. If the protection shifts inside, another rusher may get a better one-on-one. Those are possibilities, not guarantees.

The Quick-Game Problem

Now imagine the opponent doesn’t sit in long-developing passing plays. It uses quick throws to keep the rush from ever arriving.

Washington’s answer can’t simply be to send the tackle harder. The defensive front has to make the quarterback uncomfortable while the coverage takes away the easy outlets.

If the ball comes out immediately and the receiver has room to turn upfield, the rush can be right there and the defense can still lose the down. That’s why a pass rush only works as part of a coordinated plan. The pressure has to arrive at the right moment, and the rest of the defense has to make the quick answer less appealing.

And this is where Washington’s secondary matters. With Trey Amos back, and hopefully Amik Robertson healthy enough to play Thursday, the coverage can take away those quick outlets, giving the interior rush time to get home.

Patience vs. Aggression

There’s another option on the same passing down: patience instead of an all-out interior attack.

A defensive tackle can still help by controlling the middle and keeping the quarterback from stepping forward, even without sprinting straight at him. That leaves the quarterback less room to escape while the defense keeps its structure.

The trade-off is obvious. A more controlled rush lowers the chance of immediate pressure. An aggressive rush can open space if the quarterback or a blocker beats it.

Coaches have to decide which risk fits the down, the protection, and the opponent’s likely answer. This is exactly what Daronte Jones and Steve Wilks have to figure out with Newton and Barber on Thursday.

Part Eight: The Run Defense Side

Now let’s shift to early downs, when the offense hasn’t shown whether it wants to run or pass, and the ball is coming toward the interior.

Does Washington ask its interior defender to attack upfield immediately, or to hold his ground and keep the offense from gaining space between the tackles?

Option one: penetration.

On an early down, aggressive penetration can create a tackle for loss if the defender gets into the backfield before the run develops.

But it carries risk. If the defender shoots upfield and the offense runs through the space he leaves, the play can turn into a big gain before the defense can recover.

Option two: control.

The safer option is to control the interior first. The defender’s job is to avoid giving the runner a clean lane while the rest of the defense closes around the ball. That can force the runner to bounce outside, toward more defenders.

But a patient approach also gives the offensive line a chance to move the point of attack and create a lane. If you only ask your tackle to hold space, you may keep your shape and still fail to stop the run quickly enough.

The Offense’s Counters

If the offense sees Washington attacking the interior, it can call a run designed to pull the defender away from where the ball is actually going. The quarterback can also keep the ball if the defense overcommits to the running back.

If Washington plays conservatively instead, the offense can double-team the interior and keep pushing forward. Then the runner has time to find daylight, and the linebackers have to make the tackle after the offense has already gained ground.

So it’s never a choice between a good answer and a bad answer. It’s a balance between penetration and control.

What Washington Has Done Well

Here’s the encouraging part: Washington’s run defense has been one of its strengths all season. Washington hadn’t allowed a single 100-yard rusher heading into the Rams game. Through seven games, it was allowing 99.1 rushing yards per game and 3.9 yards per carry. And against the Eagles, with Payne and Kinlaw both out, Philly ran for just 91 yards.

Daronte Jones adjusted, using more four-man fronts with Tuttle at nose tackle instead of the five-man looks Washington had been running. That structural change is a big reason the run defense held up.

Now, with Newton back and Barber promoted, Jones has more bodies to rotate and more options to mix penetration and control.

Part Nine: What to Watch Thursday

Let’s bring it back to Thursday night against the Giants at MetLife.

One: Johnny Newton’s snap count. He was just activated after a chest injury. How much will he play? Is he on a snap count?

Two: Ricky Barber’s first real action of 2026. He’s been elevated before without playing. Thursday could finally be his chance.

Three: Shy Tuttle’s shoulder. He was limited this week. If he can’t go, or is limited, the rotation gets thin again.

Four: third-and-long pressure. Does Washington get interior pressure in obvious passing situations, or does it rely only on Oweh and Chaisson off the edge?

Five: the run defense. Can the rotation keep the streak of strong run defense alive against the division leader?

The Bigger Picture

The defense can only do so much. Thursday will still come down to whether the offense can score in the second half, whether Jayden takes some deep shots, and whether this team can finish a close game.

But if the defensive line holds up, with Newton back, Barber stepping in, and the run defense continuing to perform, Washington at least gives itself a real chance.
