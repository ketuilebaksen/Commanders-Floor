A conditional fourth-round pick. That’s what the longest-tenured player in burgundy and gold is worth right now, with less than three weeks left until the trade deadline.

In a minute, I’m going to show you the Adam Peters mistake that tanked Daron Payne’s value, and why I’m flipping my take today, right to your face. Then we get to the coaching decision nobody saw coming. Mike Garafolo and Ian Rapoport confirmed that Steve Wilks, a former head coach and a former Super Bowl defensive coordinator, is already working in Ashburn. But his schedule is hiding something nobody is telling you.

Welcome to Commanders Floor. Hail to the Commanders. Let’s go.

Part One: What Happened With Payne, Sources in Hand

First, the facts, with the sources right in front of you, because this channel doesn’t sell smoke.

On Wednesday, Christopher Knox of Bleacher Report put out his trade-block big board heading into Week 7. Payne is eighth out of ten. The potential buyers Knox names are the Chicago Bears and the Jacksonville Jaguars. The key line from his write-up is that if the Commanders are open to trading players, possibly for receiver help, Payne is a strong candidate.

The day before, Matt Verderame of SI dropped his list of fifteen players who could move before November 10th. Payne is tenth on that list. Verderame wrote that if Washington makes Payne available, a market should develop, and he named Detroit and San Francisco.

FanSided, also on Wednesday, had Payne sixth out of ten and named Green Bay, Carolina, and New England.

And today, Bears On SI, the SI site that covers Chicago, called bringing in Payne a no-brainer for the Bears.

Now, what’s not there? There’s no reported phone call. There’s no report that Chicago has checked in. The last time a real insider talked about Payne was Jonathan Jones of The Athletic earlier this month, and his word was that, for now, it’s quiet.

So everything you hear today about Chicago is columnist opinion. It’s an opinion being repeated a lot, but it’s still an opinion.

Part Two: The Price That Embarrassed Me

Here’s the part that has me heated.

How much does Knox say Payne is worth? A conditional 2027 fourth-round pick and/or a veteran receiver. In other words, a conditional fourth, or a veteran receiver, or both.

A conditional fourth, bro. For a guy with nine years as a starter in this league.

And what hurts most is this. According to Last Word on Sports, the previous Bleacher Report big board, from September 23rd, projected a Day 2 pick for Payne. That means a second or a third.

In four weeks, the price dropped from Day 2 to a fourth, and on top of that, the fourth is conditional.

So what happened in those four weeks? Three things.

One: the record. We went from a team thinking about the playoffs to a team at one-four-and-one. When the whole league knows you’re going to sell, buyers don’t pay you. They wait you out.

Two: the contract. According to Over the Cap, the final year of Payne’s deal voids on February 9th. Any team that buys him knows he’s a free agent in March. At most, they’re buying ten games of Payne and one playoff run. That’s it.

Three, and this is the one the people who love him don’t want to hear: this year’s numbers.

According to NFL.com, through six games Payne has fourteen tackles, one sack, and one forced fumble. The sack and the forced fumble both came in the same game, the tie with the Giants.

According to PFF, cited today by SI, he has seventeen pressures, sixteenth among interior defenders, and a 67.9 run-defense grade, nineteenth. He’s playing 74 percent of the snaps.

Against the Niners, he played 53 snaps, 85 percent of the defense’s plays, with three tackles and no sacks. Both of Washington’s sacks in that game came from other players.

Last year, in fifteen games, he had three sacks. His peak was 2022, with eleven and a half sacks and a Pro Bowl. He’s 29 years old now.

He’s not a finished player. But he’s not the Payne who scared quarterbacks four years ago either, and the market knows it.

Fam, this isn’t hate. These are the numbers every general manager looks at before he picks up the phone.

Part Three: The Money That Explains Everything

Now let me break down the contract, because this is the part almost nobody explains properly.

According to Spotrac, Payne’s cap hit this year is twenty-eight million dollars. Sixteen-point-four million of that is base salary. The rest is made up of the piece of his signing bonus that gets spread out year by year, a five-million-dollar roster bonus, per-game bonuses, and a workout bonus.

That’s why sometimes you read twenty-eight million and sometimes sixteen. Both numbers are right. One is what counts against the cap. The other is his salary.

So what happens if Washington trades him today? According to Over the Cap, if he’s traded before June 1st, Washington is left with dead cap, meaning money that counts against your cap for a player who isn’t on your team anymore. The buyer only takes on what’s left of his base salary.

Put it all in one sentence. Washington would eat roughly eleven million dollars in dead cap to get back a conditional fourth-round pick.

That’s the offer the market considers fair today.

Part Four: The Decision I Can’t Forgive

That brings us to the real mistake, because this didn’t break in October. It broke in March.

In February, Adam Peters told Commanders Wire he had a ton of respect for Daron. Nice. But in March, according to Pro Football Rumors, Peters said he wasn’t going to explore a new contract until after the season.

That’s the decision that has fans furious, and it’s the one I can’t forgive.

I’ll be fair about it. Not extending him could make sense. He was coming off a three-sack season. Handing another big contract to an interior defender who’s trending down is a real risk.

But if you’re not going to extend him, you have to sell him while he’s still worth something: in March, at the draft, or in training camp.

Peters did neither. He took the middle road, which is the worst road. He didn’t pay him, and he didn’t sell him. He let the most expensive asset on the roster walk into a contract year on a team with no Plan B. Now the market is telling him, “Give me a conditional fourth, and thanks.”

And here’s a detail that makes me laugh to keep from crying.

Do you remember who Washington sold Montez Sweat to at the 2023 deadline? Chicago, for a second-round pick. Chase Young went to San Francisco for a third. Those were two defenders in the last year of their contracts, and both brought back real picks.

Today, that same Chicago shows up as a landing spot for Payne, and the price is a fourth. Meanwhile, according to NFL.com, Sweat is serving the second game of a two-game suspension. So right now, Chicago has a hole up front.

Part Five: Why Chicago Makes Sense, for Them

Let me explain the fit, because it’s real.

The Bears are four-and-two, second in the NFC North, and they hosted New England on Thursday Night Football. Their head coach is Ben Johnson, and their defensive coordinator is Dennis Allen.

According to NFL.com, Chicago is top seven in points and yards allowed. But it gives up six yards per play, the second-worst number in the league. And according to SI, the Bears are 28th in run-stop win rate, the stat that measures how often a defender wins his block against the run.

How did they end up there? On August 30th, according to the Chicago Sun-Times, they traded defensive tackle Gervon Dexter to Atlanta for a quarterback and a fifth-round pick. Their interior group now leans on Grady Jarrett, who’s 33, with injuries thinning out the rest.

They need exactly what Payne still does well: eating blocks against the run.

So for Chicago, it’s a logical buy. For Washington at today’s price, it’s a gift.

Part Six: My Take, and Yes, I’m Flipping It

Fair warning: what I’m about to say contradicts what I said on this same channel earlier this week.

On Monday, I told you to sell Payne during the bye for a second-round pick. That was my line.

Since then, the market has answered, and the answer is a conditional fourth. So I’m changing my stance, and I’m doing it to your face.

For a conditional fourth, Payne isn’t going anywhere. Not to Chicago, not to anybody.

Here are my reasons.

One: it’s eleven million dollars of dead cap for a coin flip. A conditional 2027 fourth can end up as a fifth if the condition isn’t met. And fourth-round picks who turn into starters are the exception, not the rule.

Two: the timing. Selling Payne for that one week before you host an Eagles team without Jalen Hurts tells your locker room the season is over. And the season is not over. We’re two games behind the Giants. Payne is your best interior defender against the run. With a backup quarterback, the logical move for Philly is to run the ball. You’re going to need him on November 1st.

Three: if he walks in March, he walks. I know it’ll hurt. But if he signs a big deal somewhere else, Washington could get a compensatory pick in 2028. That pick depends on a league formula and on how many free agents Peters signs, and Peters signs a lot of them. So I’m not promising you anything. But the gap between a conditional fourth now and nothing in March is a lot smaller than it looks.

The other side, because I’m not selling you half the story.

FanSided wrote that the Commanders won’t get much in a deal. That “something is better than nothing” logic is what almost every columnist is using right now.

I get it. But “something is better than nothing” is what a general manager says after he has already lost the negotiation. And I don’t like watching Washington lose negotiations.

Here’s my line. A third-round pick or better, and you sell him tomorrow. A conditional fourth, and he stays. He plays the Eagles, the Rams, and the Giants, and if he has to go in March, he leaves with a standing ovation.

Look, I could be wrong. Maybe we get to November 10th at one-six-and-one and I’m the one begging them to sell him for whatever they can get. If that happens, I’ll say so right here. But today, with these numbers in hand, taking that offer means rewarding the mistake from March with another mistake in November.

So tell me, and don’t give me “it depends.” Payne to Chicago for a conditional fourth: sell or keep?

Part Seven: The Coaching Decision Nobody Saw Coming

If Payne stays, this defense is going to need a lot more than Payne. And Washington already made a move this week that’s bigger than it looked.

On Wednesday afternoon, Mike Garafolo of NFL Network reported that the Commanders have hired Steve Wilks to work with the defensive coaches under Dan Quinn and defensive coordinator Daronte Jones. He broke it together with Ian Rapoport, and he added two details that matter a lot.

The first: the decision to hire Wilks was made last month, and he started last week. So he was already in the building the week of the San Francisco game.

The second: Wilks will help with game planning, but he won’t be with the team on game days.

Nicki Jhabvala of The Athletic filled in the rest. She says Wilks is a part-time assistant. He doesn’t work game days. He comes in on Sundays and leaves after practice on Wednesdays.

She also passed along the official line: the hire is not a response to anything that happened in recent weeks, and it’s not an indictment of Daronte Jones as defensive coordinator.

Who Is Steve Wilks?

If you can’t quite place him: Wilks is 57, and he has done just about everything in this league.

He was defensive coordinator in Carolina in 2017. He was head coach in Arizona in 2018 and went three-and-thirteen. He was defensive coordinator in Cleveland in 2019. In 2022 he was interim head coach in Carolina, taking over a team that was one-and-four and going six-and-six the rest of the way.

In 2023, he was defensive coordinator for the 49ers, the year San Francisco won the NFC and lost the Super Bowl to Kansas City 25–22 in overtime. According to SI, that defense ranked third in points allowed, at 17.5 per game. San Francisco still let him go afterward.

In 2025, he was the Jets’ defensive coordinator until Aaron Glenn fired him in mid-December.

And for full context on Daronte Jones: this is his first year as an NFL defensive coordinator. Washington brought him in on January 30th from Minnesota, where he spent four years as defensive backs coach, to replace Joe Whitt Jr. after last season’s five-and-twelve.

The Numbers Behind the Hire

Now look at Jones’s defense.

According to Pro Football Reference, Washington is giving up 28.7 points a game, bottom four in the league. According to Yahoo, it’s allowing 354.5 yards a game, 254 of them through the air. It has eleven sacks in six games, tied for twentieth.

Then there’s one number I love, but take it with a grain of salt. According to SI, against the Niners, Washington’s defense gave up 303 yards and 17 first downs in the first half. In the second half, it gave up 72 yards. Seventy-two. And that was Wilks’s first week in the building.

Coincidence? Probably. But don’t tell me you didn’t think it.

Let me be clear, though, because I don’t want to sell you a movie. Wilks isn’t there on game days, so he wasn’t the one making halftime adjustments. That was Jones’s staff. But that week’s game plan already had his fingerprints on it.

What a Sunday-to-Wednesday Assistant Really Does

Here’s what almost nobody explained.

Sunday, Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday are the days an NFL game plan gets built. On Sunday and Monday, you study your own tape and the opponent’s. On Tuesday, you write the plan. On Wednesday, you install it at practice. Thursday and Friday are for polishing.

Wilks is there exactly on the days when you decide what you’re going to do on Sunday, and he leaves right when the reps start.

He doesn’t call the plays, but he shapes the plan. And in the NFL, the guy who writes the plan carries almost as much weight as the guy who calls it.

Part Eight: My Take on Wilks. I Don’t Buy “It’s Not an Indictment.”

I know this goes against the official version, but I don’t buy it.

To be clear, I’m not doubting Jhabvala. She’s reporting what she’s being told. What I don’t buy is the message.

If you bring in a former head coach who ran a Super Bowl defense, and you put him in your game-planning meetings while your first-time coordinator ranks bottom four in points allowed, that’s an insurance policy. Call it whatever you want.

And look at the date. The decision was made in September.

Think about what that means. This wasn’t a panic reaction to the Giants tie, the viral fourth-down clip, or Monday night in San Francisco. The front office decided, back in September, that this defense needed another experienced voice in the room. That means the concern existed before the worst of this season had even happened.

That’s not a small detail. That’s the organization admitting, quietly, that it had doubts about the setup it built in January.

Now connect it to everything else we’ve covered this week. Ian Rapoport says Dan Quinn stays, but the long-term future is still a question. Mike Garafolo says tweaks are coming before Week 8. And now a former head coach, with a Super Bowl defensive résumé, is sitting in the defensive game-planning room four days a week.

Fam, if you’re Daronte Jones, you read that as a warning. And if you’re Dan Quinn, a defensive head coach himself, you have to wonder why your bosses felt the defense needed someone beyond the staff you hired.

What I Want to See

Here’s what would convince me this is a real fix and not just optics.

Against Philadelphia on November 1st, I want to see a defense that plays the first half the way it played the second half in San Francisco. I want fewer explosive plays through the air, because 254 passing yards a game is where this defense is bleeding. And I want a plan built to stop the run, because with a backup quarterback, that’s what Philly is going to try to do.

That’s also why the Payne decision and the Wilks decision are connected. If you’re bringing in Wilks to fix the game plan, you don’t sell the one interior defender who makes a run-stopping plan work for a conditional fourth the week before the game.
