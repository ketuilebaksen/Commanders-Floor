Another game, another pile of opportunities, and once again, the Washington Commanders let them slip through their fingers.

Washington lost to the Los Angeles Rams 20–13 at home, on the day the franchise retired John Riggins’s jersey. That drops the Commanders to two-five-and-one.

And here’s the part that hurts the most. If Washington had won, and the Eagles had beaten the Giants, Washington would have gone to New York on Thursday night with a chance to take control of the division race. Instead, Washington lost, and the Giants won.

Now Washington is three games out of first place, with the trade deadline tomorrow afternoon.

So what happened? Who’s to blame? And what should Adam Peters do in the next 24 hours?

Welcome to Commanders Floor. Hail to the Commanders. Let’s go.

Part One: The Big Picture, Quickly

Let’s get the standings out of the way, because they matter.

At two-five-and-one, Washington is three games behind the Giants. The Eagles have fallen to two-and-seven, so Washington isn’t technically last in the NFC East. But realistically, this division is not producing a wild-card team. The only path to the playoffs runs through first place, and Washington is three games back with nine to play.

The chances of climbing back are very low. Not zero, but very low.

I don’t want to spend this whole video on the big picture, though. I want to talk about what actually happened in this game, because there’s a lot to break down.

Part Two: I Thought Washington Would Win

Let me take some accountability first.

I thought Washington was going to win this game. I felt it matched up well with the Rams. And in a lot of ways, the matchup played out the way we expected.

We said the Rams would use a lot of 13 personnel, with three tight ends on the field. They did.

We said the Rams’ running game wouldn’t be explosive against a Washington run defense that hadn’t allowed a 100-yard rusher all season. It wasn’t.

We said the Rams’ secondary was banged up. Trent McDuffie was out. Jaylen Watson was out. The Rams were missing both of their starting corners, just like we talked about.

So a lot of the preview was right. But the one thing that matters most, the final score, went the other way.

Part Three: Puka Nacua Took Over

Let’s start with the player who beat Washington.

Puka Nacua had fourteen catches for 150 yards and a touchdown.

Fourteen catches. Most of them came close to the line of scrimmage: quick throws, intermediate routes, screens. He wasn’t stretching the field vertically on every play. He had a couple of big gains, but mostly he just kept catching the ball, moving the chains, and breaking tackles.

And here’s the stat that should make every Commanders fan wince: in the fourth quarter, Puka Nacua had more receiving production than Washington’s entire team.

One player outgained a whole offense in the quarter that decided the game.

Remember what Daronte Jones said this week? He said Nacua is really dangerous once he has the ball in his hands, and that Washington wouldn’t have one corner follow him everywhere, because Nacua moves around the formation so much.

Well, that plan didn’t work. Washington mixed coverage on him, and Nacua found the soft spots all day. When the game was on the line in the fourth quarter, Matthew Stafford kept going to Puka, and Washington couldn’t stop it.

That’s a coaching issue as much as a player issue. If one receiver is beating you fourteen times, at some point you have to change something. Bracket him. Shadow him. Put your best corner on him. Do anything except keep doing what isn’t working.

Part Four: Jayden Daniels. Not Bad, but Not the Hero.

I know what the conversation is going to be about after this game, and who it’s going to be centered on.

When you’re a star quarterback with a big name, people are going to talk about you. And some of the instant reactions I’ve seen go something like: “Maybe Jayden isn’t who we thought he was.” “That loss is squarely on Jayden.”

So let’s look at it honestly.

Jayden’s line: 24 of 35 for 274 yards, one touchdown, no interceptions, one lost fumble. He also ran four times for 16 yards.

I don’t think Jayden played a bad game. He didn’t throw the team out of it, and he didn’t make a bunch of mistakes. He wasn’t inaccurate.

But one touchdown and one turnover isn’t going to win many games. And people will point to two plays in particular.

Play one: on the final drive, there was a throw that was nearly intercepted by Quentin Lake. Jayden and Antonio Williams were on different pages. Jayden thought Williams would sit down in the zone, and Williams kept running. By that point it was a desperation drive anyway, but it’s still a miscommunication you don’t want at the end of a game.

Play two, the big one: on a fourth-and-long, Jayden had Van Jefferson on the left side. Jefferson beat Emmanuel Forbes’s press coverage cleanly. It would have taken a great throw, a really difficult throw. But MVP-level quarterbacks make that throw.

That was Jayden’s chance to rise above everything else that went wrong in this game: the drops, the poor offensive line play, and a running game that just wasn’t there. That was the moment for the hero to step up, and he didn’t.

So Is It Jayden’s Fault?

No. I don’t walk away from this game saying the loss is Jayden’s fault.

If you think he played a bad game, respectfully, I don’t think you were watching closely. But did he play a great game? Did he make the hero play when he had the chance? No, he didn’t.

Both things can be true. He played well enough to keep Washington in it, and not well enough to win it. And when your supporting cast is struggling the way Washington’s was, your quarterback has to be great, not just good.

Part Five: The Offensive Line Was the Worst Unit

Now let’s talk about the unit I think was the worst on the field: the offensive line.

As the game went on, one player started to stand out for the wrong reasons: Brandon Coleman. He just didn’t look up to speed.

And I have to take some accountability here too. All week, I said that if there was ever a time to face Myles Garrett, it was right after he came off injured reserve. He’d barely played in more than a year, and he was limited in practice all week. I expected a rusty, limited version of him.

In the first half, I looked right. Garrett had one weird play where he stuck a hand out and batted the ball out of Jayden’s hands for the fumble. He didn’t really tackle Jayden. He barely touched him. He just got a hand on the ball, and it came loose. Other than that, he was relatively quiet in the first half.

Then the second half came, and whatever rust Garrett had disappeared entirely.

Garrett was outstanding, and he was beating Brandon Coleman badly down the stretch. According to live numbers, which haven’t been checked against the tape yet, Garrett had nine pressures in this game. I’d guess most of them came against Coleman.

So yes, I was right about the first half and wrong about the game. I didn’t think Garrett would be irrelevant, but I expected a lesser version of him. Instead, he took over when it mattered.

The Bigger Offensive Line Problem

This isn’t just about Coleman. Remember the context.

Coleman is playing left tackle because Laremy Tunsil has been out all season after tearing his triceps in training camp. He’s learning a new position in the middle of the year against some of the best pass rushers in the league. That’s an incredibly hard job.

But the results are the results. This line has struggled all season: nine different combinations through Week 5, sixteen offensive penalties on second down, and repeated breakdowns. Against an elite pass rusher like Garrett, all of those problems showed up at once.

And when the line can’t protect Jayden, you get exactly what we saw: rushed throws, a fumble, drives that stall, and no time for the deep shots that work best against a backup secondary.

Part Six: The Running Game Wasn’t There

The running game deserves its own mention, because it was bad.

With Jacory Croskey-Merritt and Jeremy McNichols on injured reserve, Washington was relying on Rachaad White and Austin Ekeler. Neither is a true every-down back, and it showed.

The Rams played a lot of zone and two-high shells, which basically invite you to run the ball. Washington couldn’t take advantage. When you can’t run against two-high looks, you become one-dimensional, and that’s exactly when an elite pass rusher like Garrett can pin his ears back.

This is why we talked about Tony Pollard yesterday. Washington needed a back who could carry the ball twenty times and punish a light box. Today showed exactly how big that need is.

Part Seven: The Emmanuel Forbes Revenge Game

There was a lot of storyline in this game, and Emmanuel Forbes was right in the middle of it.

With McDuffie and Watson out, Forbes, Washington’s former first-round pick, had to play a big role.

Forbes had his ups and downs. He gave up a touchdown to Van Jefferson, who had his own revenge storyline: Jefferson is a former Ram, playing against his old team. Funny little irony there.

And Forbes got beaten by Jefferson on that fourth-and-long that Jayden missed.

But Forbes is going to walk out of this game feeling like he won, because his team won. That’s how football works. Washington’s receivers made plays against him, but the scoreboard is what everybody remembers.

Remember, Forbes had plenty to say about Washington after he left. Today, he gets the last word.

Part Eight: The Defense Did Its Job, Mostly

Let’s give the defense some credit, because it wasn’t the main reason Washington lost.

Holding the Rams to 20 points is a solid performance. The run defense did its job again. The Rams didn’t get explosive plays on the ground, and Washington still hasn’t allowed a 100-yard rusher this season.

With Daron Payne and Javon Kinlaw both out, Shy Tuttle, Tim Settle, and Ricky Barber held up in the middle against a Rams team that loves to run.

The problem was Nacua. Fourteen catches and 150 yards. And in the fourth quarter, when the defense needed one more stop, it couldn’t get off the field.

So the defense didn’t lose this game. But it didn’t win it either, and it couldn’t solve the one player the whole league knew the Rams would feature.

Part Nine: John Riggins Day

Let’s not forget the backdrop of this game.

This was John Riggins jersey retirement day. One of the greatest players in franchise history, a Super Bowl MVP, a legend of the glory years, was honored in front of the home crowd.

Days like that are supposed to inspire. The building was electric, and the franchise was celebrating its history.

And then the team lost, again, at home, in a game it was positioned to win.

That contrast tells you exactly where this franchise is right now. The past is glorious. The present is frustrating.

Part Ten: What This Means for the Trade Deadline

Now the big question: the trade deadline is tomorrow, Tuesday, at 4 p.m. Eastern.

Before this game, we laid out two paths. Win, and Washington is three-four-and-one, a buyer with a real shot at the division. Lose, and Washington is two-five-and-one, three games out, and the conversation shifts to selling.

Washington lost. So here’s what I think Adam Peters should do.

Sell the one-year rentals for real value.

K’Lavon Chaisson: if the Patriots or any contender offers a fourth-round pick or better, take it. He’s on a one-year deal, and his value will never be higher.
Dyami Brown: ESPN’s Aaron Schatz floated him to the Rams for a Day 3 pick. With Burks and Williams emerging, a Day 3 pick for a one-year player is fine value.
Austin Ekeler: if a contender needs a veteran receiving back, listen.

But still buy what stays for years.

This is important. Losing to the Rams doesn’t change the long-term value of a young receiver. Josh Downs, whom Ian Rapoport says Washington could land for a third- or fourth-round pick, is 25. Brian Thomas Jr. is 24 and under contract through 2027 with a 2028 option. Those aren’t rentals. They’re pieces for Jayden’s window.

So the plan becomes a smart hybrid: sell what leaves in March, buy only what stays, and never give up future picks for half-season rentals.

What changes after this loss: a short-term running back like Tony Pollard makes much less sense now. Paying for half a season of a back on an expiring deal only makes sense if you’re chasing the playoffs this year. At three games back, you’re probably not.

Part Eleven: The Dan Quinn Question Is Back

We can’t avoid this one.

Remember what Jeremy Fowler reported a few weeks ago: the games before the trade deadline would shape how the team sees Dan Quinn. Those games were the Eagles and the Rams. Washington went one-and-one.

Remember what Ian Rapoport reported: Josh Harris doesn’t like firing coaches midseason, and the long-term future is still a question.

This loss doesn’t change Harris’s preference about midseason firings. Quinn is probably coaching on Thursday in New York, and probably for the rest of the season.

But the long-term question just got louder. Since the NFC Championship Game, this team is now seven wins, seventeen losses, and a tie. And the Rams loss happened at home, on a celebration day, against a team missing both of its starting corners. Those are exactly the games a coach on the hot seat has to win.

Part Twelve: Thursday Night in New York

There’s no time to dwell, because Washington plays the Giants on Thursday night, on a short week.

The Giants are now three games ahead. A Washington win would cut that gap to two and give this season one more breath. A loss would basically end any division hopes for good.

Here’s what Washington has to fix in three days:

One: protect Jayden. Give Brandon Coleman help with chips, tight ends, and quick throws.

Two: find a running game, even an imperfect one. You can’t be one-dimensional again.

Three: make the big throws. When the chance comes, Jayden has to make the hero play.

Four: finish games. Washington has been in almost every game this year. The problem has been the fourth quarter.

Final Word From Commanders Floor

Let me put it all together.

Washington lost to the Rams 20–13 at home, on John Riggins jersey retirement day, and fell to two-five-and-one, three games behind the Giants.

Puka Nacua beat Washington almost by himself: fourteen catches, 150 yards, a touchdown, and more fourth-quarter receiving production than Washington’s entire team.

Jayden Daniels wasn’t the problem, with 274 yards, a touchdown, and no interceptions. But he lost a fumble and missed the big fourth-down throw to a wide-open Van Jefferson. He was good, not great, and Washington needed great.

The offensive line was the worst unit on the field. Myles Garrett took over in the second half, with around nine pressures, many against Brandon Coleman.

The deadline is tomorrow. Sell the one-year rentals for real value, buy only young players who stay, and forget the short-term running-back fix.

And Thursday in New York might be the last real chance to keep this season alive.
