Kickers spend years learning to hit the ball as far as they can. Washington's young kicker is learning to do the opposite.

That's one of the most interesting threads from Dan Quinn's Friday press conference ahead of Monday Night Football against the Cincinnati Bengals.

The session also covered a running back who had a really good week, a starting running back dealing with heel soreness, a player who got hurt on the last play of Thursday's practice, a veteran receiver managing a lingering foot problem, and a no-huddle package that appeared for one stretch against the Giants.

And the official injury report confirmed it: Washington has real questions in its backfield heading into the biggest game of the season.

It was a short, practical session, so I'll be careful to stick to what Quinn actually said. Let's go through it topic by topic and break down what each piece means for Monday night.

Part One: The Energy

Quinn opened by talking about the environment. His message was that the team wants Northwest Stadium as loud as possible against the Bengals' offense, and that his team is ready to get rolling.

For a Monday night game, that's natural. Quinn wants his team to feed off the crowd.

And think about what's waiting on Monday. Primetime. The all-black uniforms, if the reports are right. Jayden Daniels posting "Black it out." Jonathan Allen coming home in Bengals colors. And Joe Burrow, the MVP favorite, on the other sideline.

The crowd noise matters. Against an offense that relies on Burrow's pre-snap reads and adjustments at the line, a loud stadium makes communication harder. That could be a real advantage for Washington's disguise-heavy defense.

Part Two: The Running Back Who Had a Great Week

The first injury question was about running back Jacory Croskey-Merritt, whom the team calls Bill.

Quinn's answer was positive. He said Croskey-Merritt had a really good week. The team would finish the last part of the evaluation process over the weekend, but everything had trended in the right direction.

What the Injury Report Says

According to ESPN's injury page, Croskey-Merritt is listed as questionable for Monday's game with a groin injury. He's still technically on injured reserve with a designation to return, so the team would need to activate him to the 53-man roster before kickoff.

Reading Quinn's Words

When a player returns from a long injury, the medical and coaching staff watch three things: how he handles the practice workload, how his body responds afterward, and whether he can make the explosive movements the position requires.

A coach who says a player has "trended in the right direction" is describing steady progress. A coach who says the team will "finish the process" is reserving the final decision.

So it's positive, but it's not a promise.

Why It Matters

Remember where Washington's run game has been. Croskey-Merritt went on injured reserve in mid-October and missed the 49ers, Eagles, Rams, and Giants games.

Before he went down, he was the lead back, with 239 rushing yards and two touchdowns.

Against a Bengals offense that has scored at least 31 points in five straight games, a run game that controls the clock and keeps Burrow on the sideline might be Washington's best defensive weapon.

Part Three: Rachaad White's Heel

Then came the concerning part.

Quinn said Rachaad White had soreness in his heel, so the team held him out of practice on Friday. The hope, Quinn said, was that less pounding would help him feel better going into Monday.

He explained that White was able to do some of the work earlier in the week, but as the heel got sore, the team decided to hold him out some on Thursday as well.

Quinn's Expectation

Asked what he expects for White on Monday, Quinn was cautious. The idea, he said, is to get White feeling good.

According to ESPN's injury page, White is officially questionable with a heel injury.

If White Can't Go

A reporter asked who would take the snaps if White couldn't play. Quinn said it would be a combination of players, with some used more in certain roles.

That tells us Washington has multiple options and would spread the work around. Austin Ekeler would be the obvious candidate for third-down work. And if Croskey-Merritt is activated, he'd likely handle early downs.

Why Teams Hold Players Out

Think about why a coach holds a player out of practice when the injury is soreness.

Practice involves a lot of pounding, especially for running backs. Resting a sore area for a day lowers the risk of making it worse.

A player who misses a Friday practice isn't necessarily out for the game. And Quinn's wording, "in hopes that it makes him feel better going into Monday," shows the goal is to make White available.

The Backfield Picture

So Washington's backfield heading into Monday:

Rachaad White: questionable, heel.
Jacory Croskey-Merritt: questionable, groin, still on injured reserve with a return designation.
Austin Ekeler: available.

White has been the workhorse during Croskey-Merritt's absence, with roughly 275 rushing yards on 65 carries. Ekeler has added around 109 yards on 33 carries.

The ideal scenario is both White and Croskey-Merritt playing. The worst case is neither one going, and Ekeler carrying the load against a Bengals defense that will know Washington is short-handed.

Part Four: The Player Hurt on Thursday

Another reporter asked about a player who got hurt the day before.

Quinn explained that the player was injured on the last play of Thursday's practice and was sore on Friday, so the team held him out. Quinn said he'd monitor the player over the next 48 hours.

The player's name was unclear in the source. On the official injury report, safety Quan Martin is also listed as questionable with a shoulder injury, after being limited in Thursday's practice.

If Martin can't play, that's a real concern. Against Ja'Marr Chase and Tee Higgins, Washington needs all of its fastest defensive backs.

Part Five: Reading Between the Lines

A quick note on how this session read.

Every answer was short, and Quinn gave each reporter exactly what was asked and no more. That's typical for a Friday before a game. A coach has no reason to give the other sideline extra information, and the injury designations come out anyway.

So the useful thing for a fan is to read the verbs: "held out," "monitor," "trended in the right direction," "available."

Each one carries a different level of certainty. Together, they tell you who's likely on the field.

Part Six: The Kicker Learning to Kick Short

Now the kicker, which was the most detailed answer of the session.

A reporter asked about Washington's rookie kicker, who's about halfway through his first NFL season, and what Quinn thinks of him.

Quinn said there's a lot he likes about what he's seen. He said the rookie has an excellent battery to work with: the punter, who also holds, and the long snapper.

The Counterintuitive Skill

Then Quinn said something really interesting.

For a lot of kickers coming out of college, he said, the instinct is to crush the ball and hit it out of the end zone every time.

But that's a different skill from hitting the ball so it stays in bounds, which is what Washington wants, because the team likes covering kicks.

So Quinn said he's seeing improvement in that area, with the kicker trying to put the ball down inside the five-yard line, not in the end zone, as best he can.

Quinn added that the rookie has a strong leg and that the team has a lot of confidence in him. And with kickoff rules changing so much over the last two years, he said, having the touch to know what to do and how to do it is a big part of the job.

He likes how the kicker is developing.

Why the New Kickoff Rules Matter

Let's explain why this matters so much.

Under the old rules, a kickoff into the end zone was usually a touchback. The receiving team got the ball at a set spot, and that was it.

Under the new kickoff rules, a touchback gives the receiving team better field position than it used to. So kicking into the end zone isn't automatically the safe play anymore.

A kick that lands inside the five and stays in the field of play can be covered, and that creates a chance to pin the opponent deep.

So placing the ball has become a weapon. A kicker who can only boom the ball is less valuable than one who can also place it.

Quinn Credits the Whole Unit

Notice who Quinn credited first in that answer: the battery, the punter and the long snapper, before he talked about the kicker himself.

A kicker is only as good as his snap and hold, and a young player benefits from a steady operation around him. Quinn was quietly giving a vote of confidence to the whole unit, not just the rookie. That's a distinction casual fans often miss.

Part Seven: How the Kicker Affects Quinn's Decisions

A follow-up asked whether Quinn's confidence in the kicker has changed his decisions on fourth down or field position.

Quinn said it has.

He said he felt pretty strongly about the rookie early, and gave him some 50-yard attempts right from the start of the season. He joked that he hoped one of those wouldn't be the rookie's first kick of the game, but sometimes that's how it goes.

Quinn said he likes that the kicker has kicked outdoors in places where the weather wasn't reliable, so he's prepared for it. He said he always sees the kicker's strength in practice, in his distance and his warmups.

And he mentioned that last week, after the game, the kicker stayed out in the rain to keep working, which showed he was ready for the challenge.

Why That Matters

It's interesting to hear a head coach connect his kicker to his own decision-making.

When a coach trusts his kicker from 50 yards, he can be more aggressive about attempting a field goal on fourth down instead of punting. That changes the whole shape of a drive.

Quinn giving the rookie long attempts right away shows the confidence was there from the start, and it's grown as the rookie has proven himself.

The Candid Line

One more thought. Quinn's joke about hoping a long attempt wouldn't be the rookie's first kick of the game was candid.

A coach doesn't get to choose when a rookie is tested, and the staff gave him real challenges from Week 1. So what the kicker has shown since then is a record built under pressure.

Why It Matters Monday

Against the Bengals, this could be huge.

If Washington is going to win a game where Cincinnati is scoring 31-plus points per week, it can't settle for punts in Bengals territory. A kicker who can make 50-yarders turns drives that stall at the 35 into three points.

And a kicker who can drop kickoffs inside the five gives Washington's coverage unit a chance to pin Burrow deep. Every yard Burrow has to drive is another chance for the defense to force a turnover.

Part Eight: Treylon Burks's Foot

The next question was about Treylon Burks, whose foot has been an issue.

A reporter said Burks had been seen back out at practice and asked whether he's good to go.

Quinn said he is, and that the team is close.

He explained that the team used an on-off schedule to let the foot heal: the early part of Wednesday on, Thursday off, then back on the next day. So Burks was getting plenty of speed work in practice even before a full practice.

Quinn said Burks is doing well, and that the team hopes to get him back to a regular practice schedule as soon as next week or the week after.

Pain Tolerance

A reporter asked if it's a pain-tolerance issue. Quinn said it is, but that the foot is doing a lot better, and that the medical staff and Burks have managed it well.

Quinn said it was very difficult for Burks to miss games, and that the hardest part was knowing he couldn't be himself. But Quinn said he's seeing the speed the team needs.

Why Burks Matters

Burks has become one of Washington's most important receivers since Terry McLaurin's season-ending injury.

Remember the final drive against the Giants. Burks drew a 33-yard pass interference penalty that set up the game-winning touchdown.

And against the Eagles, he had a 54-yard catch.

He's 14 catches for 260 yards this season, almost 19 yards per catch. He's Washington's downfield threat, and against Cincinnati, Washington will need explosive plays.

Managing a Nagging Injury

Managing a nagging injury is a balancing act. Too much practice can aggravate it, and too little can leave a player rusty.

The on-off schedule gives the foot recovery time without pulling Burks out of the rhythm of the week. And Quinn's comment about a regular schedule "next week or the week after" suggests the team sees the issue as manageable and temporary.

Part Nine: The No-Huddle Weapon

Then a reporter asked about the no-huddle offense.

At the end of the first quarter against the Giants, Washington went to a no-huddle, shotgun look for a five-play sequence. It worked, but the offense didn't go back to it until the fourth quarter.

Given that success, what did Quinn like about it, and why not go back to it sooner?

Quinn said it was a planned part of the game plan, something the team wanted to get to in order to move quickly. He said it'll be available in future games when the team wants to use it, and that he thinks it's a weapon worth having.

Sometimes, he said, it's done by personnel group: staying in the same group of players and going fast.

The Rhythm Factor

Quinn said that over the last couple of weeks, the offense has found a better rhythm, with players not constantly rotating in and out, and that plays into it. Keep the same group on the field and let them get going.

Why No-Huddle Could Matter Monday

There are a few reasons the no-huddle could be a big weapon against Cincinnati.

One: it limits defensive substitutions. If Washington doesn't huddle, the Bengals can't easily swap personnel to match up.

Two: it simplifies the defense. Defenses facing a fast tempo often have to play simpler coverages, which makes the pre-snap picture easier for Jayden to read.

Three: it builds rhythm for Jayden. He's at his best when he's playing fast and decisive, not overthinking.

Four: it can wear down the pass rush. Tired defensive linemen get less push.

But there's a tradeoff. No-huddle can also mean quick three-and-outs that put Washington's defense back on the field against Burrow. Against a team scoring 31-plus every week, the offense has to be careful about tempo.

My take: use no-huddle in bursts, like Washington did against the Giants. Change the tempo when the offense needs a spark, then slow it back down to control the clock.

Part Ten: The Center Decision

The session also touched on the center decision, though the source cut off before Quinn's full answer.

Here's what we know. Lucas Patrick has cleared the concussion protocol and is back at practice. And Nick Allegretti played very well at center against the Giants, allowing just one pressure according to Pro Football Focus, while delivering much cleaner snaps than Patrick had.

So Quinn has to decide: Patrick back at center, or Allegretti stays there?

I'll update you as soon as the lineup is clear. But based on how the offense looked against the Giants, I'd keep Allegretti at center and find another role for Patrick.

Part Eleven: What This All Means Monday

Let's put it all together.

The good news:

Croskey-Merritt had a really good week and could be activated.
Treylon Burks is good to go after managing his foot.
The rookie kicker is earning Quinn's trust, both on long field goals and placement kickoffs.
Patrick is cleared, giving Quinn options on the offensive line.
The no-huddle is a weapon Quinn plans to use again.

The concerns:

Rachaad White is questionable with a heel injury.
Croskey-Merritt is also questionable and still needs to be activated.
Quan Martin is questionable with a shoulder injury.
A player was hurt on the last play of Thursday's practice.

What to Watch

Two: the secondary. If Quan Martin can't play, who covers Chase and Higgins?

Three: the kicker. Will he get a long field goal attempt, and can he pin the Bengals deep on kickoffs?

Four: no-huddle. Does Washington use it early to establish rhythm?

Five: center. Allegretti or Patrick?
