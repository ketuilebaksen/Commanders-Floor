Two wins. Three losses. One tie.

That was Washington's record in one-score games over the first half of the season. And after Monday night's win over the Bengals, Dan Quinn called it the margin that had to change.

This is Quinn's postgame press conference after Washington's 22–20 win over Joe Burrow, Ja'Marr Chase, and Tee Higgins. And it's a great one, because Quinn was unusually specific about why the late-game moments went differently this time.

He explained the fourth-down decision that put the ball back in Jayden Daniels's hands. He talked about the rookie receiver who delivered, the defensive plan against two of the best receivers in the AFC, the rookie kicker who was perfect, and the offensive line racing the clock.

And on top of all that, there's breaking news on Brandon Aiyuk: he's officially a free agent, and he's visiting the Commanders today.

Welcome to Commanders Floor. Hail to the Commanders. Let's go.

Part One: The Aiyuk Update

Let's start with the news you've been waiting for.

On Tuesday, Brandon Aiyuk cleared waivers. ESPN's Adam Schefter reported it, and also said Aiyuk is expected to draw interest from a few teams, including Washington, and wants to sign soon.

So no team claimed his San Francisco contract, exactly as expected. According to Riggo's Rag, nobody was ever going to take on cap hits of more than 20 million dollars a year for the next two years off waivers.

He's Visiting Washington Today

And here's the big one. Schefter reported, per agent Drew Rosenhaus, that Aiyuk is scheduled to visit the Commanders on Wednesday. The Athletic reported the same, citing a league source.

Ian Rapoport added one more detail: Aiyuk already owns a house in the Washington area, so he wouldn't even have to move.

Where Things Stand

Let me be clear: he hasn't signed yet. A visit isn't a contract. Schefter said other teams are expected to show interest too.

But a visit the day after clearing waivers, with a house in the area, a repaired relationship with Jayden, and a general manager who was in San Francisco's front office when Aiyuk was drafted, makes this feel very close.

Rapoport reported earlier this week that the practice squad is the most likely path. As we said in the last video, I'd rather see Washington offer him the 53-man roster. Either way, we'll have it covered the moment anything is signed.

Now, let's get to Dan Quinn.

Part Two: Quinn's Opening

Quinn opened with the fans. He said the blackout uniforms and the atmosphere were a lot of fun, and he liked the energy the team built going into the game all week.

He noted that it was a Thursday-to-Monday turnaround, and that he expected the game to come down to critical situations and complementary football.

He said Cincinnati's offense was unique and tough to defend, so the game wasn't going to be Washington's defense against Cincinnati's offense. It would come down to finding ways in all three phases. And he thought that showed throughout the game, especially at the end of both halves.

His Honest Box-Score Summary

Quinn's summary was honest.

The turnover margin was zero. He was pleased that his offense protected the ball, but he thought Washington would need to create one or two takeaways to get another possession.

He said the third-down offense needs work, even though the team gained plenty of yards.

And in the locker room, he told the team it was a team win and that he was proud of them.

The Two Stars

Then Quinn named the two stars of the night.

Drew Stevens went five for five on field goals. Quinn joked that waiting for the final kick might have been the longest minute of his life.

And Antonio Williams made some big plays.

Two young players coming through at the end of the game was, in Quinn's words, a big moment for the team.

Let's Do the Math

Think about Stevens's night. Washington scored 22 points: one touchdown, plus five field goals. Fifteen of Washington's 22 points came off a rookie kicker's foot.

And remember what Quinn said on Friday about Stevens: he trusted him from long range starting in Week 1, he liked his development, and he saw him stay out in the rain after a game to keep working. On Monday night, that work paid off.

Part Three: The Fourth-Down Decision

The first question went straight to the point.

A reporter noted that Quinn had been on the wrong end of late-game situations this year, and asked how much of the improvement was coaching and preparation, and how much was just Jayden taking over.

Quinn said it was a bit of both. The scars run deep, he said, and the team has put a lot of work into those situations.

The Decision

Then Quinn laid out how he thought about the end of the game.

Washington faced a fourth down late in the fourth quarter. Quinn had to decide whether to go for it or kick.

He decided he needed to take the lead there. Washington was still above the two-minute warning, where it could use timeouts, and he didn't want a scenario where Jayden never touched the ball again.

The Most Revealing Exchange

This was the most revealing part of the press conference.

A reporter asked if part of the thinking on that fourth-and-short was to kick the field goal and make sure Jayden would get one more possession.

Quinn said yes. It was a back-and-forth decision, but he felt it was the right time to take the lead because there was still time above the two-minute warning. He said that if they hadn't been above it, he probably would have gone the other way.

And then he said the line that explains everything: Burrow is not a quarterback you want holding the ball at the end of the game.

What That Means

Think about that decision.

If Washington goes for it and fails, Burrow gets the ball with a lead and can run out the clock.

If Washington goes for it and converts, it might score a touchdown, but it also might burn so much clock that Cincinnati gets the last possession anyway.

So Quinn kicked the field goal to take a 19–17 lead, giving Cincinnati the ball but leaving enough time for Jayden to get one more chance. He'd rather have his own quarterback with the last possession than leave the game in Burrow's hands.

Remember the Fourth-Down Debate

This matters because of the fourth-down debate we covered earlier this week. Washington came into the game converting just 38 percent on fourth down this season, after 87 percent in 2024.

Some fans wanted Quinn to go for it more. But Monday night showed that sometimes the smartest fourth-down decision is not going for it. It's about managing the clock and controlling who gets the final possession.

The Zero Blitz

Quinn also talked about the Bengals' answer.

Cincinnati made a good play, he said. Washington called a zero blitz, and the Bengals ran what Quinn described as a screen-and-go down the field.

A zero blitz means everyone rushes, with no safety help behind the coverage. It's an all-in call, and the risk is that a receiver who gets past his defender has open field.

That play set up Evan McPherson's 56-yard field goal with 46 seconds left, giving Cincinnati a 20–19 lead.

Quinn admitted aggressive calls come with a price. But getting a stop with time left, so the offense could get another chance, was big, even though the decision was close.

Part Four: The Margin That Had to Change

The second question was about resilience.

A reporter said Quinn has been consistent all season, even when results weren't going his way, and that the players have kept fighting. How gratifying is it to see that turn into late-game wins?

Quinn said that over the last two weeks, it's shown up more fully than at any other time.

Then he gave the number that frames the whole season.

In the first nine games, Washington played six one-score games and went 2–3–1 in them. That was hard, he said. And that was the margin that had to change. It was one of the main things the team talked about during the bye week.

What He's Really Saying

Let me stress what Quinn is saying, and what he isn't.

He's not saying the team was bad in those games. He's saying the difference between a good record and a bad one came down to six close games, and the team lost more than it won.

When he calls it "the margin," he means the distance between the season Washington had and the season it could have had.

Flip two of those results, and Washington is 6–3–1 right now, tied with the Giants for first place.

Two Straight Close Wins

And now look at the last two weeks.

Washington beat the Giants 24–21 on a late Jayden-to-Diggs touchdown. And it beat the Bengals 22–20 on a field goal as time expired.

Two one-score games, two wins. That's the margin changing.

Quinn said he couldn't have been prouder of Jayden at the end. He thought Jayden ripped a couple of throws the way only he can. He said he didn't think anything could top last week's win, but Monday night did. He called it one of the best team wins.

Part Five: Antonio Williams's Breakout

Next, the rookie.

A reporter asked about Antonio Williams stepping up and making plays, not just at the end but throughout the game.

Quinn said it was Williams's best performance.

He mentioned one play that looked like it might have involved a hip-drop tackle, and joked that a 22-year-old Williams is much more resilient than the version of him ten years from now.

But mostly, Quinn said Williams was clutch, made big plays in big moments, and has shown that in a lot of situations.

The Full Circle

Remember the timeline.

Against the Giants, Williams played 86 percent of the snaps but got just two targets.

On Wednesday, Quinn said he wanted the ball in Williams's hands more, including on early downs. He said the staff used the bye week to figure out what unique things Williams brings.

And on Monday night, with 34 seconds left and the game on the line, Jayden went to Williams twice on long gains.

That's a coach saying something publicly and backing it up on the field.

Part Six: The Defensive Game Plan

Next, a reporter asked about Cincinnati's receivers. Much of the pregame talk was about how Washington would handle them deep, and they were relatively quiet.

Quinn first mentioned a player who was out for Washington. He said that player tried as hard as he could to get back, and if any competitor could make it back, it was him.

Then he credited defensive coordinator Daronte Jones and the defense for a nice job with their disguises.

He admitted Washington gave up one good deep ball over the top to Tee Higgins. But he reminded everyone that Chase and Higgins are the top two receivers in the AFC for a reason, and they've made a lot of big plays.

The Philosophy

Here's what I like. Quinn was specific about the plan.

A big part of it was the interior pass rush. The question was whether Washington could get pressure up the middle.

Quinn said it's tempting to play zone and play it safe against this team. But if you do, they're likely to rip you apart. So it's hard to challenge them, but in his opinion, that was what was best for Washington that night. And he knew it would take all three phases.

That's the whole philosophy in one thought. Against a great passing offense, a safe zone gives the quarterback and receivers time. The only answer is to pressure the passer and disguise the coverage before the snap.

The Coordinator Question

A reporter brought up the two new coordinators and the talk about how things would take time. The defense has improved over the last few weeks. Is this what Quinn envisioned?

Quinn said you'd like everything to be organized by the end of training camp, but sometimes that's not how it goes.

What he sees is a group that has shown resilience in learning lessons. His job, he said, is to support the coordinators at the highest level, give meaningful feedback, keep them going when things get frustrating, and keep them aggressive.

He admitted it's not easy to be aggressive against the Bengals at the end of a game, because they've made a lot of plays in those moments.

The Best Line of the Night

And then Quinn said what I think is the best line of the press conference.

He said that since they were above the two-minute warning, if they were going to fail, they should fail fast. He quickly added that he didn't mean they expected to fail. But this wasn't a game where you let the Bengals bleed out the clock and kick a field goal at the very end.

That's a coach explaining why he preferred an aggressive call that could end the drive quickly, rather than a conservative approach that let Burrow control the clock.

What He'd Like Back

Quinn said he was pleased with the defense's resilience, but there were disguises and plays he'd like back. That's what correcting and coaching is for.

Overall, though, he was pleased, and he singled out one play: Frankie Luvu's stop on fourth-and-1. Quinn said that was as big as it gets, and those are the kinds of plays you need to beat an opponent like Cincinnati.

He finished by saying the Bengals are a good team.

Part Seven: Guarding Chase and Higgins

The next question was about how Washington covered Chase and Higgins.

A reporter noticed that Washington pressed Chase more than Higgins and asked if that was intentional.

Quinn said it's a tough assignment. He pointed to all of Chase's broken tackles in previous games, because of his unique style and run-after-catch ability. So Washington wanted to stay closer to Chase, with a good tackling plan.

Quinn said he didn't know yet what the missed tackles would look like on film, but overall, he felt the defense made tackles on the completions.

Higgins did get over the top on that one deep catch. But on other occasions, Cincinnati took a shot, was forced to check the ball down, and Washington made good tackles.

Quinn said he loved the fight in it.

The Run Defense

Quinn also talked about the run game. He thought that against two-high safety looks, Washington defended some of the runs well. One Bengals runner had some good contact runs that went a couple of yards farther than Quinn would have liked, but overall, he thought it was a full team win.

"Two-high" means two safeties deep. Offenses often respond by running the ball, because there are fewer defenders near the line of scrimmage. So holding up against those runs matters a lot.

Remember Quinn's Pregame Words

Remember what Quinn said before the game: any corner wants to be in front of a receiver as often as possible, so his release goes lateral. That doesn't mean pressing every snap, but being in front makes over-the-top throws harder.

He also said the hardest plays to defend are Burrow's second-chance plays, when a play breaks down and becomes a scramble drill.

Monday night, the plan was clear: stay close to Chase, tackle well after the catch, and force Burrow to check the ball down.

Part Eight: Jayden Daniels and the Cape

Then came the most personal question: does Jayden ever surprise Quinn anymore?

Quinn said it's been cool, over their three years together, to watch Jayden grow, not just as a player but as a man in difficult circumstances.

He talked about Jayden knowing when to put the cape on. It's not on second-and-1 in the second quarter, Quinn said. It's in the fourth quarter, when the game is on the line. That's when Quinn feels Jayden fully express who he is.

And in those moments, Quinn said, Jayden looks as cool as he is, because he really is that cool.

The Calluses

Another reporter asked about the "calluses" Quinn always talks about building through end-of-game situations, and how much the early-season failures helped over the last two weeks.

Quinn said there's no question there were failures, and he thinks those scars helped.

When Washington got the stop it needed, the team knew it needed first downs and clock management, what Quinn called "chunks and clock." And if there was a chance for Jayden to have the ball at the end, that would be huge.

Quinn said the team has spent a lot of time on situational football, and he called Monday night the best example of complementary football at the end of both halves.

The End of the First Half

Quinn described the end of the first half, while hedging a bit on his memory. He thought it was a touchdown by Bill, meaning Jacory Croskey-Merritt, scored with enough time left that Cincinnati didn't get the ball back with a real chance.

So Croskey-Merritt not only came back from injured reserve, he scored Washington's only touchdown. That's a huge return.

Quinn said he knew the game would be punch, counterpunch, punch, counterpunch, because of who the Bengals' weapons were.

Part Nine: The Race Against the Clock

The final drive deserves its own section.

A reporter described the last two plays as a moment of incredible poise by the offensive line. After the two big completions to Williams, the linemen had to sprint 20 yards, and then 40 yards, to get set so Jayden could spike the ball.

And on the second sprint, if they moved too slowly, there could have been a 10-second runoff, which would have ended the game.

The reporter asked what was going through Quinn's mind.

Quinn said he was looking up at the clock and then at the players as they sprinted. As they got closer, he knew they'd be able to clock it, but for a stretch he didn't know how fast they'd get there.

That's "chunks and clock," he said. When it's a 20-yard play, you have to haul it to get there. And that was a big emphasis for the team.

Since Washington started the drive without timeouts, Quinn said, you're even more alert to the clock, because it's your only option.

Why That Matters

Think about that.

Offensive linemen weigh 300-plus pounds. After a long completion, they have to sprint downfield, line up, and get set before the clock runs out.

That's not luck. That's practice. And it's exactly what Quinn meant when he said the team has put a lot of work into those situations.

Nick Allegretti, Sam Cosmi, Brandon Coleman, and the rest of the line sprinting 40 yards to let Jayden spike the ball might be one of the most underrated moments of the season.

Part Ten: The Defense's Growth

The final question was about the defense's improvement. How much of it is the players knowing the defensive language better in Week 11 than in Week 1?

Quinn said it does matter, and that he wishes it came right away, but it doesn't.

He thinks Washington's defense is much better now than it was in Weeks 1 and 2. He pointed to tackling, communication, how to pressure, and how to handle opponents' screens and bubble passes. It took going through the process.

He likes where the players are now, especially with a cornerback out. He said Rasul Douglas responded to the challenge, along with two other players, depending on which package Cincinnati used.

Preparing for Every Look

Cincinnati used a lot of three-receiver sets, Quinn said. The question was what it would look like against two- and three-tight-end sets, and he thought the defense was prepared.

He knew it would be a battle with the Bengals' offense clicking, but he liked that Washington made it hard on them early. When Cincinnati chose to take the ball first and Washington got a stop, Quinn said, that helped, and he liked seeing that fight.

The Defensive Turnaround in Numbers

Remember the numbers from earlier this month. In the first five games, Washington allowed 24 or more points every time.

Since then: 49ers, 23; Eagles, 10; Rams, 20; Giants, 21; Bengals, 20.

That's five straight games without allowing 24 points. And the Bengals came in having scored at least 31 in five straight games.

Final Word From Commanders Floor

If you step back, this press conference was a coach explaining a change in approach.

The fourth-down decision: Quinn kicked the go-ahead field goal above the two-minute warning on purpose, so Jayden would get the ball back if Burrow answered. As Quinn put it, Burrow is not a quarterback you want holding the ball at the end of the game.

The margin: Washington went 2–3–1 in one-score games over the first nine games. In the last two weeks, it's 2–0. That's the margin changing.

The stars: Drew Stevens went five for five on field goals, and Antonio Williams had his best game, delivering the two biggest catches of the season.

The defense: Daronte Jones's disguises, an interior pass rush, staying close to Ja'Marr Chase, and Frankie Luvu's fourth-and-1 stop.

Jayden: he knows when to put the cape on.

And Aiyuk: he's a free agent and visiting Washington today.

Washington is 4–5–1. The Giants are 6–3–1. Seven games left. Next up: at Arizona.
