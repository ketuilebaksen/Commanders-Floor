Nine seconds left. Ball at the Rams’ 31-yard line. Down 20–13. And Washington throws a checkdown over the middle.

That’s how Sunday ended. And according to Ian Rapoport, the owner had already warned Ashburn weeks ago that some wins had to come.

So tonight, let’s do what nobody else is doing. Let’s put ourselves inside Josh Harris’s head, follow the clues the insiders have given us, and figure out what he might do next. I’ll also tell you the drastic move I’d actually make, and why the best one for Jayden Daniels isn’t the one everybody’s yelling about.

Plus, John Riggins watched his number 44 go up into the rafters and said something that’s splitting the DMV. And Adam Peters is running out of time before the trade deadline. Does he sell Stefon Diggs?

Welcome to Commanders Floor. Hail to the Commanders. Let’s go.

Part One: Picture Yourself as Josh Harris

Picture yourself as Josh Harris on Sunday night.

You’re up in your suite in Landover. You just hung a legend’s number in the rafters. You’ve invested billions of dollars in this franchise. There’s a new stadium on the way at the RFK site. And you have the most important quarterback this team has had in thirty years.

And you just watched your offense throw a checkdown with nine seconds left at the 31-yard line.

What are you thinking? What do you do the next morning?

You don’t have to guess. You can read the clues. Harris doesn’t say much in public, but over the last month, the insiders have sent some very clear messages about where his head is. When you line those messages up next to what happened on Sunday, you realize the clock has already started.

Part Two: What the Insiders Said in October

Let’s go in order, because every line matters.

October 17th, after the Giants tie: according to Bleacher Report, Tom Pelissero said Harris has never believed in midseason coaching changes, but that he’d be paying close attention to the Monday night game against the 49ers.

October 18th: according to Rotoworld, Mike Garafolo of NFL Network reported that Harris would love for Quinn to stay on as head coach.

October 19th: according to Bleacher Report, John Keim of ESPN said Quinn’s future was not in immediate jeopardy, and that ownership was aware of the support Quinn has in the locker room.

October 21st, the one that matters most: after the bye, Ian Rapoport posted that Josh Harris doesn’t like firing a coach midseason, and that the long-term future is still a question. Then came the line almost nobody read: some wins do have to come.

October 27th: six days later, Rapoport turned it up. Going through the coaches on the hot seat, he named Dan Quinn as the other name to watch. He said Quinn seemed to have bought himself some time, with a condition: if the performance doesn’t improve and the wins don’t come for Washington, things change.

We broke all of that down right here back then. And I’ll say today what I said then: that wasn’t a vote of confidence. It was a loan.

Part Three: What’s Happened Since

Since that report, Washington has played two games.

It beat the Eagles 27–10 at home, and it was a good win. Then on Sunday, it lost 20–13 to the Rams.

One-and-one. Did “some wins” come? One did.

But the way Sunday’s game was lost is exactly the kind of loss an owner looks at under a microscope.

Part Four: How the Rams Game Slipped Away

Washington led 10–6 at halftime.

In the third quarter, safety Nick Cross intercepted Matthew Stafford. That was the perfect moment to put the game away.

What did the offense do with it? According to ESPN, four plays, nine yards, and a field goal.

According to ClutchPoints, Washington went three of twelve on third down.

According to the AP, Washington had two possessions in the final minutes, and neither ended in points.

And the last one: according to Athlon, Washington had the ball at the Rams’ 31-yard line with nine seconds left. Jayden threw it short over the middle. According to Riggo’s Rag, it was a checkdown to Chig Okonkwo. Then came two laterals that ClutchPoints called hapless, and the play died before it reached the 20.

Quinn’s Explanation

According to JP Finlay, Quinn said the short pass happened because the Rams blitzed, and that he wasn’t happy with the execution on that play.

This is where I grab my head.

With nine seconds left and the ball on the 31, there’s no play in any NFL playbook where the winning option is a short pass over the middle. If the plan against the blitz was to dump it off five yards downfield, the problem isn’t execution. It’s design.

Look, I could be wrong. Maybe there was a deep read that the pressure took away. But when the head coach talks about execution, he’s saying, without saying it, that it’s on the players. And an owner who’s paying close attention hears that.

Part Five: The Line That Worries Me More Than the Play

Here’s what Quinn said after the game, according to the AP, in my own words: it was a frustrating game, the team once again hasn’t nailed its complementary football, he’ll keep saying it until they do, and now that they’re halfway through the season, it all has to click.

Let’s be real. Complementary football, the idea that offense, defense, and special teams feed off each other, is the right idea. But if you have to repeat it every week, it’s because it isn’t happening.

And “we’re halfway through the season” is exactly what an owner thinks when he pulls up the standings. Halfway through the season, that line stops being a diagnosis and starts sounding like an excuse.

Look at the third quarter on Sunday. That’s complementary football failing in real time. The defense hands you an interception, and the offense gives back nine yards and a field goal. That’s the opposite of complementary.

The Pattern at the End of Games

The end-of-game problems aren’t new either.

Against the Giants earlier this season, according to The Score, Washington let the clock run out in overtime and walked away with a tie. Remember the headset throw and the pylon kick. Remember the in-week checkpoints Quinn promised to tighten.

And on Sunday, nine seconds and a checkdown.

That isn’t bad luck. That’s preparation. And preparation is on the head coach.

Part Six: Thinking Like Josh Harris

Now think about it the way Harris would.

Not like the angry fan in the parking lot in Landover, but like a guy who made his fortune buying companies, fixing them, and making decisions without emotion.

First, the standings. According to The Score, the Giants are five-two-and-one and lead the NFC East. Washington is two-five-and-one, three games back.

Second, the schedule. Next up is Thursday night at MetLife Stadium against those same Giants, on a short week.

And Jayden Daniels, the franchise quarterback Harris will have to pay, already said it after the game, according to The Score: next week is a must-win.

Third, Harris’s history. We have to be fair here.

In 2023, Harris’s first year as owner, the team fell apart. Washington sold Montez Sweat and Chase Young at the trade deadline, and Ron Rivera still stayed until the end. Harris fired him on January 8th, 2024, the Monday after the final game. Not a day earlier.

But be careful. Harris’s group also owns the New Jersey Devils, and in March 2024, the Devils fired head coach Lindy Ruff in the middle of the season.

“Doesn’t like” is not the same as “never.”

Part Seven: The Three Paths on the Table

So what can Harris do? Let me keep it simple. He has three paths.

Path one: do nothing.

That’s what his history says, and it’s what Rapoport reported. Quinn stays, and everything gets evaluated in January.

To me, that’s the most likely outcome this week.

Path two: the truly drastic decision. Fire Quinn midseason.

It goes against everything reported in October. I don’t think it happens this week.

But if Washington loses on Thursday at MetLife and falls four games out of first place, the conversation gets real. At that point, Rapoport’s line about how “some wins do have to come” stops being a warning and becomes a countdown.

And remember what we talked about a few weeks ago: if a coaching change ever happens midseason, running backs coach Anthony Lynn, a former Chargers head coach, has been mentioned by Front Office Sports as a potential interim option.

Path three: the one nobody’s asking for, and the one I’d push for. A change in the middle.

Without firing anybody, Quinn stops running end-of-game situations the way he runs them now, and David Blough hands Jayden control of those moments. Let him read it. Let him decide.

According to the numbers in my source, a quarterback who hasn’t thrown a single interception all season has earned the keys.

Think about it. The end-of-game problems have come from the sideline: the clock management against the Giants, the headset confusion on fourth down in San Francisco, and now a checkdown with nine seconds left. Meanwhile, Jayden has protected the football all year.

Giving Jayden more control at the end of games would be the smartest drastic decision Harris could push for, and the cheapest. It doesn’t require firing anyone. It just requires trusting your franchise quarterback.

Part Eight: My Verdict

Is Harris losing his patience? Here’s my read.

I’m not in that office, and my read could be wrong. But Harris’s patience came with a condition, and Rapoport spelled it out: the wins have to come.

One came in two games. And the one that got away on Sunday got away because of the same old problems: the end of games, the clock, and an offense that goes quiet right after the defense hands it a takeaway.

For me, Thursday is the line. Not because I say so, but because Jayden said so, and because it’s exactly the kind of game an owner uses to decide whether what he’s seeing is a rough patch or a pattern.

Win in New York, and the gap is two games. Quinn buys more time, and the season breathes.

Lose in New York, and the gap is four games, the season is effectively over, and the long-term question stops being a question.

Part Nine: The John Riggins Moment

While the offense was fading out on the field, something happened at halftime that put Harris in the middle of another conversation.

At halftime, Washington retired John Riggins’s number 44. The AP photo shows him speaking at the ceremony in Landover, with Washington leading 10–6.

It’s the seventh retired number in franchise history. It joins:

Sammy Baugh’s 33
Bobby Mitchell’s 49
Sean Taylor’s 21
Sonny Jurgensen’s 9
Darrell Green’s 28
Art Monk’s 81

Then came the Washington Times headline that’s already going around everywhere: Riggins, the man they call “The Diesel,” vowed during the ceremony that he’ll die a Redskin, on the very day the team was honoring him.

Who Is John Riggins?

For those who joined this fan base more recently, here’s why that number means so much.

With this franchise, Riggins piled up 7,472 rushing yards, 79 rushing touchdowns, and 1,988 carries. All three are franchise records.

He was the MVP of Super Bowl XVII, with 166 yards on 38 carries. And he ran the famous 43-yard touchdown on fourth-and-inches with Washington trailing 17–13, the play that still shows up in every video about this team’s history.

If you’ve ever wondered why your granddad gets emotional about a running back wearing number 44, that’s why.

The Controversy

Riggins has never hidden how he feels about the name change. In 2022, when the team became the Commanders, he told NBC Sports Washington that he felt completely disenfranchised, cut off, as if somebody had taken something that was his.

And on Sunday, with a packed stadium and his number hanging there forever, according to the Washington Times, he said it again.

Let me be clear before I give my take: what I have is the headline and the photo. I don’t have the full speech word for word, so I’m not going to make up quotes. If the full video comes out, we’ll break it down together.

My Take on Riggins

I know I’m going to get comments from both sides on this.

Riggins has every right in the world to say it. He played in that helmet. He won a Super Bowl with that name on his chest. For a man born in Kansas in the 1940s who was the heart of this team in the 1980s, that’s his identity as a player. You’re not going to ask a 77-year-old man to change how he feels at a ceremony that’s literally about his career.

And the team did the right thing by letting him speak. When Harris announced the honor back in July, he said something I liked: the fans didn’t just admire Riggins, they identified with him. If you identify with somebody, you don’t censor him on the day you honor him. You let him be himself.

But here’s the part some of you won’t like: the name isn’t coming back, and I think that’s a good thing. This franchise made a decision, stuck with it under new ownership, and is building a new identity around a new stadium and around Jayden Daniels.

Riggins represents the glory of the past. Jayden represents the hope of the future. Both can be honored at the same time.

Part Ten: The Deadline. Does Peters Sell Stefon Diggs?

Now let’s get to the clock that’s ticking even faster than Quinn’s: the trade deadline is Tuesday at 4 p.m. Eastern.

And the biggest name on Washington’s possible sell list is Stefon Diggs.

Let’s lay out the facts we’ve covered on this channel. Diggs is turning 33. He’s a free agent in March. He leads the team with four receiving touchdowns. Jeremy Fowler has mentioned him as a name that could come up at the deadline. And there’s been talk about a Rams interest angle, since Los Angeles lost Davante Adams to a hamstring injury, though that was only a proposal floated by FanSided, not a report.

The Case for Selling Diggs

At two-five-and-one and three games out, Diggs is a rental for a team that probably isn’t making the playoffs.

Every target that goes to Diggs is a target that doesn’t go to Antonio Williams or Treylon Burks, the young receivers who will be here in 2027. We’ve watched Williams and Burks grow into bigger roles over the last few weeks. Giving them even more of the offense for the rest of the season is how you find out exactly what you have.

And if a contender offers a Day 2 or Day 3 pick for a receiver who leaves in March anyway, that’s value Washington loses forever if it waits.

The Case for Keeping Diggs

The counterargument is real too.

Diggs is Jayden’s most experienced target, and he’s been the team’s best red-zone receiver. Selling him days before a must-win game against the Giants sends a message to the locker room that the season is over.

And if Washington wins on Thursday and cuts the gap to two games, you’d regret selling.

My Verdict on Diggs

Here’s where I land.

If a contender offers a third-round pick or better, Diggs goes. That’s real value for a 33-year-old receiver on an expiring deal.

If the best offer is a sixth or seventh, he stays. That isn’t worth the locker-room message before Thursday.

And whatever happens with Diggs, keep chasing a young receiver. Josh Downs, whom Ian Rapoport says Washington could land for a third- or fourth-round pick, or Brian Thomas Jr. Selling an old receiver and buying a young one is exactly how you rebuild without giving up.

Part Eleven: What Thursday Means for Everybody

Let’s bring it all together, because Thursday night at MetLife ties every storyline together.

For Dan Quinn: it might be the most important game of his tenure. Win, and he buys more time. Lose, and the “countdown” becomes very real.

For Jayden Daniels: he called it a must-win. Franchise quarterbacks own moments like this. He needs to make the hero play he didn’t quite make against the Rams.

For Josh Harris: it’s the evidence. Rough patch or pattern? Thursday will help him decide.

For Adam Peters: whatever happens at the deadline on Tuesday, Thursday tells him how much patience this roster has earned.

Final Word From Commanders Floor

Let me put it all together.

The clues: in October, insiders said Harris doesn’t believe in midseason firings, would love for Quinn to stay, and was aware of his locker-room support. But Rapoport added that some wins had to come, and later put Quinn on his list of hot-seat coaches.

The results since: one-and-one. A win over the Eagles, then a 20–13 loss to the Rams that slipped away through the same end-of-game problems, including a checkdown from the Rams’ 31 with nine seconds left.

The three paths: do nothing, which is the most likely outcome this week. Fire Quinn midseason, which isn’t likely now, but becomes real if Washington loses Thursday. Or the smart middle path: give Jayden control at the end of games.

John Riggins had his number 44 retired and, according to the Washington Times, vowed to die a Redskin. He has every right to feel that way, and the franchise is right to keep moving forward.

The deadline: sell Diggs only for a third or better, and keep chasing a young receiver.

And Thursday at MetLife decides a lot more than one game.
