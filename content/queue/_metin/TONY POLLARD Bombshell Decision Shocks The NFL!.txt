The NFL trade deadline is two days away, and the Washington Commanders have a big choice to make before Tuesday at 4 p.m. Eastern. Do they buy? Do they sell? Or do they try a little bit of both?

One position could force the issue: running back.

Injuries have thinned out the backfield, and Washington is still very much in the race for an NFC East title that’s wide open. That makes one name worth serious consideration: Tennessee Titans running back Tony Pollard.

Let me be clear from the first sentence: this is not a report that a deal is happening. Nobody has said Washington has called Tennessee. But Pollard could fill a clear need if the price is right, and today’s game against the Rams could decide whether Adam Peters goes for it.

Welcome to Commanders Floor. Hail to the Commanders. Let’s go.

Part One: The Backfield Has Been Hit Hard

Let’s start with the problem, because it’s gotten worse.

Jacory Croskey-Merritt is on injured reserve. He was Washington’s leading rusher, with 239 yards, and the 2025 seventh-round pick who became one of the best stories on this team.

Jeremy McNichols has now joined him on injured reserve with a midfoot injury.

That leaves Rachaad White and Austin Ekeler holding down the fort. Craig Reynolds is on the practice squad, and Robert Henry Jr. was just re-added.

So there are options, but the group has been cut down at exactly the moment Washington needs reliable contributors.

And the concern isn’t just how many names are available. It’s what kind of players they are. Washington’s current backfield features two receiving backs and no obvious every-down threat.

White and Ekeler are good at catching passes, working in space, and handling third downs. But who carries the ball twenty times on a cold December Sunday when you need to grind out a lead? Ekeler is 31. White has been more of a dual-threat change-of-pace back. Neither profiles as a true bell cow.

A team needs players who can contribute in different situations, and injuries make that flexibility even more valuable. If Washington is serious about contending for the division, it has to ask whether this backfield can carry the workload over the stretch ahead.

Part Two: Enter Tony Pollard

Tony Pollard could give Washington a different kind of option.

He’s been one of the league’s most underrated running backs for years, and right now he’s on pace for his fifth straight 1,000-yard rushing season. Five straight.

That consistency stands out even more because he’s doing it in Tennessee, on a team whose season isn’t going well. The Titans are struggling, and Pollard keeps producing.

How His Role Has Changed

Pollard’s role has changed a lot over his career.

Early on, in Dallas, he was mostly used as a third-down specialist and change-of-pace back, a guy who could catch passes and hit big plays in space.

In Tennessee, he’s become a bell cow, handling a much heavier rushing workload.

This season, the Titans have basically taken him out of the passing game. He has 133 rushing attempts and only fourteen receptions.

So while his history includes being a dual-threat back, this year’s production is coming almost entirely on the ground.

Why That Fits Washington

For Washington, that’s actually perfect.

Washington already has receiving backs in White and Ekeler. What it doesn’t have is a back who can take on meaningful rushing work, carry the ball between the tackles, and wear down a defense. Pollard has shown he can do exactly that.

And his experience as a receiver gives the offense another dimension, even if Tennessee hasn’t used it much this year. Imagine Pollard on early downs, with White and Ekeler rotating in on passing downs. That’s a complete backfield.

The argument isn’t that Pollard solves every offensive problem. It’s that he’d provide an experienced lead back while the roster is short on healthy options.

Part Three: Would Tennessee Even Listen?

There are real reasons to think the Titans might.

First: Tennessee is already open for business. Just over a week ago, the Titans traded Jeffery Simmons to the Chicago Bears for two first-round picks and a fourth. When a team trades its best player, its highest-paid player, a franchise cornerstone, it’s telling the league it’s rebuilding.

Second: Pollard is in the final season of the three-year deal he signed in 2024. A rebuilding team doesn’t usually want to pay a running back in his late twenties when its next contender might be two or three years away.

Third: the 2027 draft. Everybody around the league says it’s one of the deepest classes in years, and sellers want as many picks in it as possible. Even a mid-round pick in that draft has real value.

None of that makes a trade inevitable. But it makes it reasonable for Washington to find out what Tennessee would want.

Part Four: A Good Fit Isn’t Automatically a Good Deal

That last part matters most.

A good fit is not automatically a good deal. Washington should explore the possibility, but the price has to make sense.

Pollard’s age and contract suggest he shouldn’t cost a ton. Running backs on expiring deals rarely bring back big hauls. But Washington should confirm that instead of assuming it.

Remember the market we’ve seen over the last two weeks:

DeForest Buckner, a three-time Pro Bowler on an expiring deal, went for a fourth-round pick.
Quenton Nelson, an eight-time Pro Bowler on an expiring deal, went for a third and a fifth.

Expiring veterans are going for Day 2 and Day 3 picks. A running back on an expiring deal should cost even less.

If Tennessee asks for more than Washington believes half a season is worth, Peters has to be willing to walk away.

Part Five: What About Croskey-Merritt?

There’s another piece to this: Jacory Croskey-Merritt.

He’s on injured reserve, and it’s uncertain how long he’ll be out. Even when he returns, there’s no guarantee he’ll step right back into a major role.

He’s still part of Washington’s long-term plans. That 2025 seventh-round success story isn’t going anywhere. But it doesn’t erase the short-term problem.

That distinction matters. Going after Pollard wouldn’t mean giving up on Croskey-Merritt or deciding he has no place in this offense. It would mean recognizing that Washington has lost two running backs to injured reserve and has limited depth.

Washington can value a young player’s future while still looking for help to get through the present.

And if Croskey-Merritt comes back healthy in December, now you have Pollard, Croskey-Merritt, White, and Ekeler. That’s a deep backfield for a playoff push.

Part Six: The Offensive Line Problem

Let’s keep expectations realistic, though, because the problem isn’t only the running backs.

Washington’s offensive line makes life tough for anyone in the backfield. We’ve talked about it all season: nine different line combinations through Week 5, sixteen offensive penalties on second down, Brandon Coleman learning left tackle in the middle of the season, and Laremy Tunsil out since training camp.

A new running back would be running behind that same line, and there’s no guarantee Pollard’s arrival suddenly transforms the run game.

Even David Blough declined to comment when asked about the offensive line’s alignment this week. Everybody knows the line needs to be better.

Still, if Pollard can produce in Tennessee, on a struggling team behind a line that isn’t elite either, it’s reasonable to think he could do the same in Washington. That’s what makes this a sensible conversation rather than a guaranteed fix.

Part Seven: The Short Commitment Limits the Risk

The contract timeline could be the most attractive part.

If Washington acquires Pollard, the commitment is only half a season. If the production doesn’t justify another contract, Adam Peters can move on after the season.

That doesn’t make a trade free or riskless. Washington would still have to decide whether what it gives up is worth it. But a short window makes this much easier to consider than a long-term commitment.

There’s a big difference between adding a temporary solution and making a major long-term investment.

Washington would be looking for help during an important part of the season while its injured backs’ timelines remain uncertain. If Pollard produces, he stabilizes the room. If he doesn’t, Washington isn’t locked into anything beyond this season.

And Washington can afford it. According to Over the Cap, the Commanders have about 25.5 million dollars in cap space, the sixth-most in the league. Pollard’s remaining salary for half a season is easy to absorb.

Part Eight: Pollard vs. Jonathan Taylor

You might be wondering: what about Jonathan Taylor?

We covered it last week. The Colts are open to trading Taylor, but they want a Christian McCaffrey-style package, four picks. According to Tom Pelissero, they already turned down an offer that included a 2027 second-round pick plus another selection. And Washington isn’t on the list of teams reported to have called.

Taylor is the better player, no question. He’s 27 and under contract through 2028. But the price is enormous.

Pollard is the realistic option. He’s on an expiring deal, on a rebuilding team, at a much lower price, and he fills the same need on early downs.

If I’m Adam Peters, here’s how I see the running back market:

Taylor: only if the price drops to a second plus a Day 3 pick, which looks unlikely.
Pollard: a Day 3 pick, a fifth or sixth, would be great. A fourth is the ceiling.
Nothing at all: acceptable, if neither price works and you trust White, Ekeler, and Croskey-Merritt’s eventual return.

Part Nine: Running Back Isn’t the Only Need

Let’s keep perspective, because running back isn’t Washington’s biggest deadline priority.

Receiver comes first. Terry McLaurin is out for the season. Ian Rapoport reported Washington could land Colts receiver Josh Downs for a third- or fourth-round pick, and a final offer may come after today’s game. Brian Thomas Jr. is still in play from Jacksonville.

Defensive tackle is also thin, with Daron Payne and Javon Kinlaw both likely out for the season. Ricky Barber was promoted from the practice squad, and Johnny Newton is working his way back. So far, no defensive tackle trade has been reported.

Running back is third on the list, but it’s real.

So here’s my priority order for Adam Peters before Tuesday:

A young receiver, like Downs or BTJ, who helps Jayden Daniels for years.
A cheap, short-term running back, like Pollard for a Day 3 pick.
Defensive tackle, only if something cheap and obvious appears.

If Peters can land a receiver and Pollard without giving up a Day 2 pick for Pollard, that’s a great deadline for a team trying to win a division.

Part Ten: Today’s Game Decides It

All of this hinges on what happens today against the Rams.

Win, and Washington is three-four-and-one, two games behind the Giants with nine to play, in a division where Dallas is three-and-five and Philly is two-and-six. In that world, adding Pollard for a Day 3 pick is exactly the kind of smart, short-term move a contender makes.

Lose, and Washington is two-five-and-one. A running-back rental makes much less sense, and the focus shifts to selling one-year players.

And today’s game is a test of the running game too. The Rams average 125.5 rushing yards per game, led by Kyren Williams and Blake Corum. Their defense is tough up front, with Byron Young rushing the passer at an elite rate. If White and Ekeler can’t get anything going on the ground today, the case for Pollard gets even stronger.

So watch the running game closely. How many yards does Washington get on the ground? Can White or Ekeler handle twenty carries? Does Blough have to abandon the run? The answers will tell you how badly Washington needs help.

Part Eleven: My Take

Here’s where Commanders Floor stands.

Make the call to Tennessee. It costs nothing to ask. Washington has two running backs on injured reserve, two receiving backs left, and no true every-down back. That’s a real need.

But only at the right price. A fifth or sixth is a great deal. A fourth is the ceiling. Anything more than that for half a season of a running back on an expiring deal, behind this offensive line, and you walk away.

And don’t let it distract from the real priority. A young receiver for Jayden comes first. If Peters has to choose between Downs or BTJ and Pollard, he chooses the receiver every time.

If the price is right, Pollard could give Washington the experienced lead back it needs for the stretch run. If the price isn’t right, Washington survives with what it has and waits for Croskey-Merritt to return.
