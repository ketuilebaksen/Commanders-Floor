There’s a change coming to Washington’s defensive sideline tonight. On paper, it might look like a small adjustment. But it has already raised a much bigger question: what does it say about the people responsible for fixing this defense?

Dan Quinn is moving Steve Wilks, the consultant Washington hired just weeks ago, into a much more visible role. Tonight against the Philadelphia Eagles, on Sunday Night Football at Northwest Stadium, Wilks is expected to be on the sideline.

The surprise isn’t just that he’ll be there. It’s that this was never the plan.

Part One: The Plan That Changed

Let’s rewind, because the timeline matters here.

A couple of weeks ago, Mike Garafolo and Ian Rapoport of NFL Network broke the news that Washington had hired Steve Wilks. He’s a former NFL head coach and defensive coordinator, brought in to work with the defensive staff under Dan Quinn and defensive coordinator Daronte Jones.

At the time, the details were very specific. Nicki Jhabvala reported that Wilks was a part-time assistant. He would come in on Sundays, help build the game plan, leave after practice on Wednesdays, and not work on game days.

The official line was also very specific: the hire was not a response to anything that had happened in recent weeks, and it was not an indictment of Daronte Jones.

Now, according to reports, his responsibilities are expanding to include the sideline on game day.

Jhabvala underlined the point: having Wilks on the sideline wasn’t part of the plan when Washington hired him. This isn’t Wilks carrying out a role that was laid out from the start. The team changed his role.

That change is what turned a staffing update into a story about confidence, accountability, and the state of this defense.

Part Two: “Not a Very Good Look”

Grant Paulsen of 106.7 The Fan, who first reported the change, didn’t hold back. He called the move not a very good look for Dan Quinn.

Paulsen’s concern is that having Wilks on the sideline makes both Quinn and first-year defensive coordinator Daronte Jones look like they’re under scrutiny. He compared it to being on a sort of probation, with another coach effectively standing there checking whether the job is being done right.

And he argued that this raises an uncomfortable question: why would a team need that kind of oversight if it believed its defense was working?

It’s a tough way to read it, but it explains why the timing matters so much. Quinn is trying to fix a defense that has struggled all year, and this move comes after high-profile mistakes in game management. Think about the clock at the end of the Giants tie, the fourth-down clip in San Francisco, and moments when even Jayden Daniels looked confused about the situation.

To be fair, nothing says Wilks’s new role is specifically about those moments, or that he’s connected to every problem on this team. But it adds to the sense that Washington is searching for answers in more than one area, and that scrutiny is landing on the coaching staff as the pressure builds.

Part Three: An Indictment of Jones Too?

The criticism isn’t aimed only at Quinn.

Paulsen also described the decision as an indictment of Daronte Jones. Washington is now in its second defensive coordinator in as many years. If that coordinator needs another former defensive coordinator standing next to him to get the unit right, that’s a hard look for a first-year play-caller.

JP Finlay of NBC4 saw it as a sign of urgency. He said the move shows how desperate Quinn is to get wins.

Finlay also raised a separate but related question: should Jones have been allowed to hire more of his own assistant coaches?

That one matters. If a defensive coordinator doesn’t control enough of the staff around him, can he really install the system he wants? And when a defense is struggling, does changing the support structure help, or does it blur the lines of responsibility even further?

Part Four: The Numbers Behind the Pressure

The numbers explain exactly why all these questions are being asked.

According to Pro Football Reference, Washington ranks 31st in the league in points allowed and 25th in yards allowed. In points allowed, that’s almost dead last, at 28.7 per game.

That’s a tough return for a team that invested heavily in its defense this offseason.

Look at who Adam Peters and Dan Quinn brought in:

Odafe Oweh, signed to a four-year, hundred-million-dollar deal.
K’Lavon Chaisson, on a one-year, twelve-million-dollar deal.
Tim Settle and Charles Omenihu on the defensive line.
Leo Chenal at linebacker.
Nick Cross at safety.
Amik Robertson at corner.

On top of that, Washington used the seventh overall pick in the 2026 draft on linebacker Sonny Styles, and by most accounts, Styles is already showing signs of greatness.

But the defense as a whole hasn’t come close to his standard.

That contrast is hard to ignore. There’s new talent at every level of this defense, and the team clearly made a huge effort to strengthen the unit. Yet the rankings say the results haven’t caught up with the investment.

Part Five: Why It Hits Quinn Harder

For Dan Quinn, this gap is a big problem, because of who he is.

Quinn built his reputation as a defensive coordinator, first with the Seattle Seahawks during the Legion of Boom years, and later with the Dallas Cowboys. He’s not just being judged as a head coach trying to make changes. He’s a coach whose defensive résumé shaped everybody’s expectations when he arrived.

And so far in Washington, he hasn’t built a strong defense. In 2025, Washington had one of the worst defenses in the league. In 2026, after all that investment, it’s still near the bottom in points allowed.

With the unit there, the question isn’t simply whether Washington has enough talent. It’s whether the scheme, the communication, the coaching structure, and the execution are coming together in a way that lets that talent succeed.

Bringing in a defensive consultant, and now putting him on the sideline, is Quinn admitting the answer is “not yet.”

Part Six: Is Steve Wilks the Answer?

Here’s the other thing to remember: Wilks isn’t a guaranteed solution either.

He’s 57 and has done a bit of everything in this league. He was a defensive coordinator in Carolina and Cleveland, head coach in Arizona, and interim head coach in Carolina in 2022, where he took over a one-and-four team and went six-and-six the rest of the way.

His best recent year was 2023, as defensive coordinator of a 49ers team that reached the Super Bowl. That defense ranked third in points allowed. But San Francisco let him go after just one season.

Then in 2025, he was the Jets’ defensive coordinator until he was fired in the middle of the season.

So Wilks brings a lot of experience and perspective, but his recent results don’t automatically settle the debate. Washington isn’t adding a miracle worker. It’s adding another experienced voice.

Part Seven: Two Ways to Read This Move

There are two ways to read what Quinn is doing.

Reading one: Wilks provides experienced help at a moment when Washington badly needs fresh ideas and more support for its defense. In this version, Quinn is being humble and practical. He has a problem and he’s using every resource available. Good coaches ask for help.

Reading two: changing Wilks’s role this quickly suggests the staff is scrambling for answers under pressure. In this version, it’s a sign that the original setup isn’t working, and that the head coach doesn’t fully trust it.

Here’s the thing: both readings can be true at the same time. A sideline presence can be meant to help Jones and the players, and it can still raise questions about whether Quinn believes his current defensive operation is working the way he planned.

Part Eight: The Real Problem Might Be the Secondary

Let’s dig into the football side, because I think there’s a specific reason Wilks is getting closer to the action.

When Daronte Jones came to Washington, his challenge was clear: build a defense using sophisticated pressure concepts and disguises, ideas he learned while working with Brian Flores in Minnesota.

The warning back then was that Jones shouldn’t be expected to simply become another Flores. And now the concern is that Washington’s ambitious approach may be asking too much of its players, especially when they have to show one look before the snap and then shift into something else after it.

That complexity shows up most in the secondary, where this defense has had communication breakdowns all season. When defensive backs aren’t on the same page, a coverage can fall apart even if the original call was sound.

And there’s a coaching-structure story behind that.

Mark Bullock, who writes on Substack, laid it out. Last season, Joe Whitt Jr. was the defensive coordinator. Whitt has a defensive-backs coaching background. Jason Simmons was the pass-game coordinator, and Tommy Donatell was the defensive-backs coach.

Then Whitt and Simmons both left. Jones arrived, and Washington tried to hire a defensive-backs coach from LSU, but that didn’t work out. According to Bullock, that left Jones and Donatell to handle those responsibilities themselves.

So think about what that means. The defense lost two experienced secondary minds, never fully replaced them, and installed a complex disguise-heavy scheme that depends on the secondary communicating perfectly. No wonder there have been breakdowns.

That’s one theory for why Wilks is working so closely with Jones: the defensive coordinator may need more help coaching the defensive backs, and Wilks, with his long résumé, can provide it.

Part Nine: My Take

Here’s where Commanders Floor stands.

Remember what I said when Wilks was hired? I said I didn’t buy the official line that it wasn’t an indictment of Daronte Jones. I said bringing in a former head coach and Super Bowl defensive coordinator to sit in the game-planning room, while your first-year coordinator ranks near the bottom of the league, is an insurance policy, whatever you call it.

Well, the insurance policy just got activated.

Moving Wilks to the sideline on game day is exactly what I was worried about. It doesn’t automatically mean Jones is on his way out. But it does mean the organization wants an experienced voice right there in the moment, during the game, where adjustments actually happen.

And here’s the part that might surprise you: I actually like the move for tonight.

Yes, it’s a bad look for Quinn and Jones. Yes, it raises questions about the structure Washington built in January. But at one-four-and-one, I don’t care about looks. I care about wins.

Look back at San Francisco. Washington’s defense gave up 303 yards and 17 first downs in the first half, then only 72 yards in the second half. That was Wilks’s first week in the building, but he wasn’t on the sideline for those halftime adjustments. Now he will be.

If having an experienced former head coach on the sideline helps the secondary communicate, helps Jones adjust faster, and helps this defense avoid the second-half collapses that have haunted it all year, then it’s worth the bad optics.

But there’s a condition. The roles have to be clear. One play-caller. One voice in the defensive huddle. If Jones calls the defense and Wilks is a second set of eyes, great. If there’s confusion about who’s really in charge, that’s exactly the kind of communication breakdown this defense can’t afford.

Part Ten: What to Watch Tonight

Here’s what I’ll be watching on the defensive side tonight against the Eagles.

One: the secondary. Are the communication breakdowns gone? Are the corners and safeties on the same page when Philly motions or shifts? With Tanner McKee at quarterback, the Eagles won’t win with complicated reads. They’ll try to win with busted coverages, so don’t hand them any.

Two: the halftime adjustments. With Wilks on the sideline for the first time, does this defense come out of the locker room better than it went in? Philly has scored just six points in six third quarters this season. If Washington’s defense dominates the third quarter tonight, that’s a sign.

Three: the run defense without Kinlaw. Javon Kinlaw is a long shot to play. Daron Payne is questionable with a foot injury. If the interior is thin, can this defense still stop the run? Philly is going to try, especially with a backup quarterback.

Four: disguises that work. Jones’s scheme is built on disguise. Against a young quarterback like McKee, those disguises should be a weapon, not a liability. If the defense shows him one look and plays another without breaking down, McKee could have a long night.

Five: Sonny Styles. The seventh overall pick is the best player on this defense. Get him around the football, and let him make plays.

Part Eleven: The Bigger Picture for Quinn

Let’s zoom out one more time, because this move connects to everything we’ve been talking about.

Ian Rapoport reported that Josh Harris doesn’t like firing coaches midseason, and that Quinn is expected to stick around for the foreseeable future, though the long-term future is still a question.

Jeremy Fowler reported that the games before the November 10th trade deadline will shape how the team views Quinn.

Mike Garafolo reported that tweaks were coming during the bye.

And now, the first visible tweak is a former head coach standing on the defensive sideline.

That’s the context for tonight. Quinn has the bye, the extra preparation, the home crowd, a four-and-a-half-point line in his favor, and a banged-up Eagles team with a backup quarterback. Now he also has an extra experienced voice on the sideline.

Every resource has been provided. If the defense still struggles tonight, there will be very few explanations left.
