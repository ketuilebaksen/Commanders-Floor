Terry McLaurin is gone for the season. The bye week is ticking away. The trade deadline is getting closer every day. And one name keeps coming back to Ashburn, over and over: Brian Thomas Jr.

Jayden Daniels’s old teammate. The guy who caught seventeen touchdowns at LSU during the Heisman season. A Pro Bowler as a rookie, now buried on Jacksonville’s depth chart, with insiders saying the phones are about to start ringing.

Is it a guaranteed fix? No. Does it come with risk? Absolutely. But with the bye giving Washington a rare moment to look at its roster with fresh eyes, Adam Peters should at least make the call. And by the end of this video, I’ll tell you exactly why he should go further than that.

Part One: How Washington Got Here

Let’s take a step back and look at how this season got us to this point, because it’s been a story of missed chances and mounting problems.

Washington opened the year with losses to Philadelphia and Dallas. Then came a win over Seattle that offered a little reason for optimism, but that momentum didn’t last. Indianapolis beat us. Then came the 27–27 overtime tie with the Giants, the game that ruined Josh Harris’s week. And last Monday night, a 23–17 loss in San Francisco.

That leaves Washington at one-four-and-one heading into the Week 7 bye.

The record is hard enough to look at, but the injury situation makes it worse. Terry McLaurin isn’t just another receiver on this roster. He’s Washington’s most established veteran wideout: a guy who wins on the outside, earns his quarterback’s trust, and gives you a dependable option when a play breaks down. Losing him for the season takes away a huge part of this offense’s identity.

It also makes life harder for Jayden Daniels, who is still developing and needs real playmakers around him if this franchise wants him to take the next step.

Part Two: The Good Sign Nobody Is Talking About

There was at least one encouraging sign in that loss to San Francisco, and I don’t want it to get lost.

Jayden threw for 300 yards and two touchdowns against one of the toughest defenses in the league, and he did it after losing Terry on the second possession.

That matters. It tells you this offense hasn’t run out of ideas or ability. The arm is there. The legs are there. The fight is there.

But one strong statistical night doesn’t erase the bigger concern. Washington now has to produce like that week after week without its leading veteran receiver. That’s where this decision becomes so important.

Part Three: What’s Left in the Receiver Room

Washington does have players who can contribute, and I’m not going to pretend otherwise.

Stefon Diggs brings experience and route-running ability. Tight end Chig Okonkwo can be a weapon in the middle of the field. Antonio Williams, Treylon Burks, and Dyami Brown could all be asked to take on bigger roles.

The team doesn’t have to give up on those guys or assume they can’t help. But asking them to collectively replace McLaurin’s impact is a completely different challenge from asking them to contribute in a supporting role.

A coaching staff can shuffle targets, adjust formations, and design plays to help the players already on the roster. Those changes are worth making. But adjustments have limits.

If opposing defenses don’t believe Washington can threaten them outside and downfield, they’ll squeeze the space around the intermediate routes. They’ll put more bodies in the passing lanes Jayden wants to attack, and they’ll dare our receivers to win underneath. Every completion gets harder, and every drive depends more on Jayden creating something after the play breaks down.

That’s a lot to put on a young quarterback, especially in a season when his development has to keep moving in the right direction.

Part Four: Jayden Can’t Rescue Every Snap

This is the heart of it for me.

Jayden has the arm and the mobility to extend plays, and the San Francisco game showed what he can do when the passing attack finds a rhythm. But the goal shouldn’t be for him to rescue every possession by himself.

The goal should be an offense that gives him clear answers, several different kinds of receiving threats, and chances to use his skills without turning every snap into a spectacular escape act.

That’s what a true outside receiver does. He changes how a defense approaches the entire game, even when he doesn’t get the ball. The mere possibility of a deep completion affects where the safeties line up and creates room for everybody else. If defenders have to respect a player who can track a ball over the top, they can’t crowd the short and intermediate areas as aggressively. That helps the quarterback, the other receivers, and the tight end working underneath.

Washington needs that balance now more than ever, and that’s exactly why Brian Thomas Jr. makes so much sense.

Part Five: Why BTJ Fits

The basic football fit is easy to picture.

BTJ is a long-striding receiver who can accelerate downfield, track deep throws, and compete for the ball at the catch point. Those traits would let Washington threaten defenses vertically instead of relying on a collection of short routes and improvised plays.

And the connection to Jayden isn’t just a convenient storyline. They played together at LSU in that explosive 2023 offense, when Thomas finished with 68 catches, 1,177 yards, and seventeen touchdowns, leading the entire FBS in receiving touchdowns.

That season showed exactly why a team could bet on his upside. He turned downfield chances into big plays, and his production forced defenses to account for him on every snap.

Jayden was part of that same environment, so there’s real familiarity between them. They’ve shared a huddle, they’ve had a productive connection, and they wouldn’t be starting from zero on understanding what works between them.

Part Six: Let’s Be Honest About the Risk

Now let me be fair, because this channel doesn’t sell you a fairy tale.

A reunion wouldn’t instantly recreate what happened in college. The NFL is a different level, the systems are different, and the passing game around them would be different. Familiarity helps, but it’s not a guarantee. Thomas would still have to learn the playbook, build NFL timing with Jayden, and earn his opportunities on the field.

Washington shouldn’t trade for Thomas out of nostalgia, or assume the LSU connection automatically turns him into a star. The case has to be built on his current talent, his age, his skill set, and how well he fits a clear need.

And the recent numbers are exactly why this is a complicated decision.

In 2024, as a rookie, Thomas had 87 catches, 1,282 yards, and ten touchdowns. That was a Jaguars rookie record, and he made the Pro Bowl.

In 2025, his production dropped to 48 catches, 707 yards, and two touchdowns.

Through six games this season, he has nine catches for 105 yards and one touchdown. Jacksonville has built its passing game around Parker Washington and Jakobi Meyers, and BTJ is playing only 43 percent of the snaps. In his last two games, he has zero catches on five targets. Against Houston, he played fifteen snaps and didn’t come back after halftime.

Those aren’t the numbers of a player dominating the league. They raise real questions about consistency, about his role, and about whether that rookie production can come back.

Part Seven: Why the Bad Numbers Are Actually the Opportunity

Here’s the flip side, and it’s why I’m so interested.

Those disappointing numbers could turn BTJ into a buy-low opportunity.

Washington wouldn’t be trading for a receiver coming off a season where every team expects to pay a premium. It would be betting that the talent is still there, and that a new situation helps him find a consistent role again.

That’s a real bet, not a sure thing. But it’s exactly the kind of bet a team should consider when it has a glaring need, a young quarterback to support, and a deadline window that’s closing.

Look at the timeline. BTJ was a Pro Bowler under one staff. In 2025, Liam Coen arrived in Jacksonville with a new staff and a new system, and BTJ went dark. That doesn’t look like a player who lost his talent. It looks like a player who doesn’t fit what his coach wants. ESPN’s Dan Graziano said it plainly last week: Thomas might not fit in Jacksonville.

And there’s more on the reporting side. Jeremy Fowler of ESPN reported that several NFL executives believe Jacksonville will seriously consider trading him before the deadline. Pro Football Rumors noted that Adam Schefter and Ian Rapoport expect calls about BTJ to pick up. According to Connor Hughes of SNY, the Jaguars were already prepared to trade him in the offseason, but the offers weren’t good enough.

The Sporting News, working off Graziano’s analysis, listed the landing spots that make sense: San Francisco, Las Vegas, the Giants, and Washington. And ClutchPoints put a price on it: a 2027 third-round pick and a conditional 2028 fifth.

Part Eight: Washington Has to Do Its Homework

The key question is whether BTJ’s decline reflects a lasting limitation, or a stretch of production that doesn’t match his ability.

To answer that, Washington has to do real homework. Study his recent routes. Study his usage, and the quality and timing of the targets he’s been getting. Study how he responds when the ball actually comes his way. Look beyond the highlight reel and beyond the stat line.

And yes, find out what happened against Houston. Why did he play fifteen snaps and not come back after halftime? Nobody has really explained it publicly. Washington’s doctors need to check him out before anything is signed.

If the evaluation says his movement skills and his ability to win downfield are still there, then those recent numbers have opened the door to a smart offer.

And the money makes it even better. According to Spotrac, BTJ is on a four-year rookie deal worth 14.66 million dollars, all of it guaranteed. He counts just under four million against the cap this year and about 4.67 million next year, and there’s a fifth-year option for 2028. If Washington trades for him at the deadline, Jacksonville keeps carrying the signing-bonus money, and Washington only pays what’s left of his salary this season.

That’s a potential number-one receiver for Jayden for the next three seasons, at the price of a backup.

Part Nine: The Ideal Version of This Offense

The ideal version of this move isn’t complicated.

Diggs works the intermediate areas, giving Jayden a seasoned target in the rhythm of the passing game. Thomas stretches the coverage and gives the offense a real chance to attack vertically. Okonkwo benefits from all the space created underneath. And the rest of the receivers get to contribute without carrying the entire burden of replacing McLaurin.

That mix would give offensive coordinator David Blough more ways to build route combinations and create favorable matchups. It would give Jayden more options before the snap and during the play.

Here’s what that looks like in practice. If a defense commits extra attention to the deep threat, there’s room underneath. If it focuses on controlling the middle, Thomas gets a chance to make a play outside. And if the coverage breaks down because Jayden escapes the pocket, which happens all the time, Thomas’s ability to track a deep ball gives him a target downfield.

Not every one of those outcomes would happen right away. But adding a different kind of receiver forces the defense to account for more possibilities, and that’s huge.

Washington’s offense can’t afford to become predictable. When a team loses a player like McLaurin, opponents will test the replacements. They’ll challenge the remaining receivers to prove they can win consistently, and they’ll make Jayden hold the ball longer while he waits for routes to develop. Add someone who changes the shape of the coverage, even a player still working back to his best form, and it gets much harder for defenses to sit on the same areas of the field snap after snap.

Part Ten: What This Trade Would NOT Do

Let’s be clear about this part.

Brian Thomas Jr. would not fix the offensive line. He wouldn’t solve the defensive problems, and he can’t change the games already played. One receiver can’t turn a one-four-and-one team into a contender by himself. Washington would still need better execution, healthier players, and consistency across the whole roster.

Fans should be careful about treating any one acquisition as a magic answer.

But a trade doesn’t have to solve every problem to be worth it. It can fix one urgent weakness and give the team a better chance to compete. With Terry gone, that weakness is staring us in the face.

Part Eleven: It’s Really About Jayden

This is the part that matters most to me.

Washington has to think about more than the standings, even though the standings are impossible to ignore. This organization has to think about Jayden’s development and the environment around him.

If you believe in your young quarterback, giving him real receiving options has to be part of the plan, even in a season that hasn’t gone the way anybody hoped.

There’s a big difference between protecting a young quarterback and demanding that he make the most of a depleted group with no outside help. Jayden needs chances to learn, make decisions, build chemistry, and respond to tough situations. But that development gets undermined if the offense keeps lacking the personnel to run what it wants to run.

A true vertical receiver wouldn’t take the pressure off Jayden, and it shouldn’t. But it could create a more balanced environment, where he can see how a defense reacts and make decisions with more options available.

And this one has a bonus no other receiver on the market has: they’ve already done it together, at the highest level of college football, in a season that ended with Jayden holding a Heisman Trophy.

Part Twelve: Why the Bye Week Matters So Much

That’s why this bye week is so valuable.

Washington has a chance to review the first six games, find exactly where the offense is falling short, and decide whether the current group can handle a bigger workload. It’s also a chance to evaluate options outside the building, before the deadline closes the door.

The calendar is clear. The Eagles come to Landover on November 1st. The Rams are on November 8th. The trade deadline is November 10th. Every day that passes is a day closer to the window shutting.

And remember, Washington isn’t the only team interested. Kansas City and Pittsburgh are also in the market for receivers, and Jeremy Fowler has mentioned the Chiefs as a possible top contender for BTJ, especially with their Tyreek Hill reunion stalled. If another team moves first, this opportunity disappears.

Part Thirteen: My Take

Here’s where Commanders Floor stands.

Make the call, and make it this week. Do the homework on the tape and the medicals. If they check out, pay the ClutchPoints price, a third and a conditional fifth, without blinking.

Would I go higher? Yes. For a 24-year-old receiver on a fully guaranteed rookie deal through 2027, with an option for 2028 and existing chemistry with your franchise quarterback, I’d go up to a second-round pick. That’s not reckless. A Day 2 pick is a coin flip on a prospect. BTJ is a prospect who has already produced 1,282 yards in this league.

And I’ll keep connecting the dots like we have all week. If you need to balance the books, look at what walks in March: Stefon Diggs to a contender like the Rams, who just lost Davante Adams; K’Lavon Chaisson on his one-year deal; Daron Payne if someone offers a Day 2 pick. Sell what leaves, buy what stays. That’s how you rebuild without giving up.

Look, I could be wrong. Maybe the medical checks raise a red flag, or Jacksonville asks for a first. In either case, you walk away. But if the price is reasonable and Adam Peters doesn’t even make the call, that’s when I’m going to be upset.

Final Word From Commanders Floor

Washington is one-four-and-one, Terry McLaurin is gone for the season, and Jayden Daniels just showed in San Francisco that he can still carry this offense: 300 yards and two touchdowns against an elite defense. But he can’t do it alone every week.

Brian Thomas Jr. is the most logical answer on the market. He’s young, he’s cheap, he’s under contract, and he already has chemistry with Jayden. His recent numbers are a real concern, but they’re also the reason he could come at a buy-low price.

He won’t fix everything. He would fix the most urgent thing.
