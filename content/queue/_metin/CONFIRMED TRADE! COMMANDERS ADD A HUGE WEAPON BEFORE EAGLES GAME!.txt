Josh Harris had an entire bye week to fire Dan Quinn, and he has made his call. Ian Rapoport just confirmed it.

But there’s one line buried at the end of that report that almost nobody caught, and I’m going to show you why it should keep Dan Quinn up at night. On top of that, Quinn just sent Jayden Daniels a public message. A Grant Paulsen stat says the real problem is somebody else. And John Keim found the number that explains this one-four-and-one record better than anything. Spoiler: it isn’t David Blough.

Welcome to Commanders Floor. Hail to the Commanders. Let’s go.

Part One: Inside Josh Harris’s Head

Put yourself in Josh Harris’s shoes for a second, because the math isn’t hard.

You bought this team in 2023. You fired Ron Rivera. You hired Adam Peters and Dan Quinn. In year one, you made the conference championship game, the best season this franchise had seen in more than thirty years.

And now? Your team is one-four-and-one. There’s a viral clip of your head coach that, to half the country, looked like he didn’t know what down it was. For the second straight week, the whole league is laughing at Washington. And you’ve got a full bye week with no game on the schedule.

That is the classic moment to fire a coach in the NFL. It has always been the bye.

This morning, Ian Rapoport spoke, and I want to walk you through exactly what he said, because every word matters.

According to Rapoport, the team keeps fighting, and it’s clear Quinn has not lost the locker room. Josh Harris does not like firing a coach in the middle of a season. Based on the effort on the field, Quinn is expected to stick around for the foreseeable future. And then, at the very end: the long-term future is still a question.

So for everybody who showed up with a shovel in hand: no. Dan Quinn is not going anywhere. Not this week, and probably not this season.

I’ll be honest with you. Part of me felt relief, and part of me got mad, both at the same time. Let me explain why.

Part Two: The Report, Line by Line

Rapoport doesn’t write anything by accident. So let’s go through his report one line at a time.

Line one: “Did not lose the locker room.”

This is the line that carries the most weight, and it makes sense. Washington went down twenty to three at halftime in San Francisco and ended up losing by six. The defense gave up one field goal in the entire second half. A team that has stopped believing in its coach doesn’t do that at Levi’s Stadium against one of the best teams in the conference. That’s real, and you have to give Quinn credit for it.

But listen to me. “Didn’t lose the locker room” is the lowest bar a head coach can clear in this league. It’s like saying a restaurant is good because nobody got food poisoning. Players still running hard for you is the minimum. It isn’t a reason to stay. It’s only the absence of a reason to go right now. Those are two very different things.

Line two: “Josh Harris does not like firing a coach midseason.”

This one has history behind it, and the history backs Rapoport up.

In 2023, Harris’s first year as owner, the team fell apart. At the trade deadline, Washington sold off Montez Sweat and Chase Young, and Ron Rivera still stayed until the very end. They finished four-and-thirteen, and Harris waited until January 8th, 2024, the Monday after the final game, to fire him. Not one day earlier.

But be careful here, because “doesn’t like” is not the same as “never.” Harris’s group also owns the New Jersey Devils, and in March of 2024 the Devils fired Lindy Ruff in the middle of the season. So Harris’s ownership group has already fired a coach midseason. What Rapoport is describing is a preference, not a law, and preferences change when the record gets worse.

Line three, the hidden one: “The long-term future is still a question.”

Every headline today says some version of the same thing: Quinn survives, Quinn stays, Harris backs him.

But Rapoport did not say Harris backs him. He said Harris doesn’t like firing coaches midseason, which is a different statement. Then he added that the long term is still a question.

My read, and I’m labeling this clearly as my opinion: that is not a vote of confidence. It’s a vote of patience. Harris is telling the world, through Rapoport, that he isn’t making the move now, but he isn’t signing off on 2027 either.

If I’m Dan Quinn, that last line keeps me up at night more than any viral clip ever could.

Part Three: The Tweaks

Then Mike Garafolo, also from NFL Network, added a piece I think matters even more. He’s reporting that Washington is going to make some tweaks before Week 8.

He didn’t say what they are, and I’m flagging that because it’s one source with no details. But think about what a tweak can even be at this point. You’re not rebuilding the whole scheme in ten days. You’re not pulling a new coordinator out of thin air.

What you can change is who calls the plays, who’s on the headset at the end of games, and how the clock gets managed.

And there’s precedent for that in this franchise, under this same owner. In November of 2023, after the Thanksgiving beatdown in Dallas, Rivera fired defensive coordinator Jack Del Rio and took over the defense himself. That was a midseason tweak. It didn’t save the season, but it sent a message.

The Numbers Harris Is Staring At

Here are the numbers on Josh Harris’s desk, and they aren’t pretty.

According to Riggo’s Rag, this team has won three of its last seventeen games. We’re dead last in the NFC East at one-four-and-one. In this week’s power rankings, Mike Florio of Pro Football Talk put us at number thirty and wrote that Quinn’s time is up. There’s no wiggle room in that line.

On the other side, here’s what Quinn has going for him. The division is so broken that the Giants are leading it with only three wins. We’re two games out of first place with eleven games left, and Philadelphia is coming to our house on November 1st.

So the season is not over. Not even close. Keep that in mind, because it’s the reason Harris can justify waiting.

Part Four: Where I Land

I know this is going to split the comments, but here it is.

I think Josh Harris is making a mistake.

Not because Dan Quinn is a bad coach. He took this team to a conference championship game, and nobody can take that away from him.

He’s making a mistake because he’s picking the option that hurts the least today, not the one that helps Jayden Daniels the most.

Think about it this way. If the long term is already a question, like Rapoport says, then Harris is seriously considering not having Quinn in 2027. And if you’re seriously considering that, every week that passes is another week of Jayden’s rookie contract burned with a staff that might not be here next year.

That window, the window of a good quarterback on a cheap contract, is the most valuable thing this franchise owns. And right now, we’re spending it to buy time for a coach we haven’t committed to.

Look, I could be wrong. Maybe the bye fixes things. Maybe Garafolo’s tweaks are exactly what this team needs, and we beat the Eagles. If that happens, I’ll come right back here to Commanders Floor and say so.

But to me, keeping Quinn without changing anything real is worse than firing him. It leaves you with a coach stuck in limbo, a locker room reading the same reports you and I are reading, and an owner who has already told everybody he’s still evaluating.

My condition: if Quinn stays, the tweaks have to be big and visible against Philadelphia. Not a practice adjustment. Something you can actually see on the field on November 1st. If we don’t see it, then for me, Rapoport’s “still a question” answers itself.

Tell me in the comments: is Harris right to wait, or is every week with Quinn a week of Jayden we’re never getting back?

Part Five: Quinn’s Message to Jayden

Speaking of Jayden, Quinn said something this week that, if you listen closely, points straight at his quarterback.

He told John Keim of ESPN on Tuesday that, like most things with Jayden, there are some plays where you say that’s an incredible throw, and there are other ones where he’d love Jayden to stay through the look. There was a curse word in the middle of that quote, and I’ll spare you.

What he’s saying is simple. With Jayden, there are plays where you go, “What a throw.” And there are others where you wish he’d stay in the read, not leave early and not abandon the play that was designed.

Now, to be fair, on the fourth-and-two at the San Francisco thirteen, the throwaway with the flag in the air, Quinn actually defended Jayden. His point was that even when you know there’s a penalty, you still take your shot, because you don’t know whether the flag will get picked up. He didn’t put that throw on Jayden.

But that first line is a different story. That’s a head coach, in the week when everybody is talking about his job, saying publicly that his quarterback leaves the read too early.

What “Stay Through the Look” Means

For anybody who isn’t deep in the jargon, here’s what it means.

Every pass play has a progression: first read, second read, third read, and sometimes the checkdown. Before the snap, the quarterback reads the coverage and figures out what the defense is giving him. After the snap, he moves his eyes from one receiver to the next based on what he sees.

Staying through the look means trusting that progression all the way through. If the first guy is covered, you go to the second, then the third. You don’t take off or freelance the moment something isn’t clean.

Jayden wins a ton outside of structure. That’s what makes him special. The problem is that when he leaves structure too early, the receivers who were about to come open on the second or third read never get the ball. Against a defense like San Francisco’s, which plays a lot of zone and practically invites you out of the pocket, that kills you.

The Mixed Message

There’s something strange here, and I want to point it out.

In that same conversation, Quinn said that against the Niners’ zone, he wanted to see more scramble-to-throw, meaning getting out of the pocket in order to throw. And he said he expects the out-of-the-pocket plays to keep coming, with Jayden still running keepers.

So let me get this straight. Stay in the read. Also get out of the pocket to throw. Also keep running.

Fam, those are instructions pulling in opposite directions. When a second-year quarterback hears all of them at once, the most likely outcome is that he plays with doubt.

Quinn also said part of the bye-week self-scout is communicating more with Jayden. Good. But first, the message has to be one message.

Part Six: The Grant Paulsen Number

This is where the number comes in, the one that I think explains this whole story best.

Grant Paulsen of 106.7 The Fan posted that Washington is in the shotgun 66 percent of the time, the sixth-highest rate in the NFL. Under center? Only 27 percent. That’s 30th in the league.

Why does that matter so much?

Because under center is where real play-action lives. That’s where the running back and the quarterback sell the run with their backs to the defense, and the linebackers take that one step forward that opens up the windows behind them.

It’s also where the bootleg lives. The bootleg gets Jayden out of the pocket on purpose, with a two- or three-man read all on one side of the field. In other words, it’s how you get the Jayden who runs and the Jayden who reads in the same play.

And we barely use it. We’re thirtieth.

On Monday in San Francisco, the prettiest play of the game was a Jayden rollout with a twenty-nine-yard completion to Chig Okonkwo. Designed movement, a simple read, a big gain. That is exactly what this offense should be doing ten times a game, and it isn’t.

The Controversial Part

I know half of you are going to hate this, but let’s keep it real. Quinn is right. Jayden leaves the read too early. We all see it. There are plays where the second receiver is breaking open and Jayden is already heading for the sideline.

Being our franchise quarterback doesn’t make him untouchable. If we want him to become a top-five quarterback in this league, that habit has to go.

But now think about who builds Jayden’s game plan.

If you know your quarterback tends to bail on the read early, the fix isn’t telling a reporter about it. The fix is designing an offense that makes it easier for him to stay in the read. That means more under center, more play-action, more short half-field reads, and more plays where the first guy is actually open.

Nobody on this staff is doing that. The scheme pulls him out of the read, and then the head coach asks him in public to stay in it.

So yes, Quinn is right about Jayden. That’s exactly why it’s so serious, because it’s a criticism that also lands on his own staff. If the diagnosis is right and you haven’t changed the scheme in six weeks, the problem isn’t the quarterback.

Part Seven: The Number That Explains One-Four-and-One

If you want to know how much all of this is costing us on the field, John Keim found the exact number, and it’s worse than you think.

In this week’s ESPN Power Rankings, Washington is at twenty-nine, down one spot from last week. Every beat reporter had to pick the one problem that best explains their team. John Keim picked third-and-long.

Here are the numbers. Washington has had eighty-one third downs this season. Forty-eight of them came with seven or more yards to go. Of those forty-eight, we converted ten.

Let’s do the math together. Forty-eight out of eighty-one is almost sixty percent. So six out of every ten times we reach third down, we’re in a spot where almost no team in the NFL converts consistently. And ten out of forty-eight is roughly twenty-one percent. One in five.

Over at Fox, Ralph Vacchiano had us at twenty-seven with another stat: Washington converts 30.9 percent of its third downs, the second-worst rate in the league.

Put those two numbers together, and this is my math, not theirs, and something interesting falls out. 30.9 percent of eighty-one is about twenty-five conversions. If ten of those came on third-and-long, the other fifteen came on the thirty-three short or medium third downs. That’s close to forty-five percent. Not elite, but competitive.

What does that tell you? The problem isn’t really third down. The problem is how we get to third down.

The Heart of It: Second Down

Keim has a second number, and I think it’s the heart of all of this.

Washington has sixteen offensive penalties on second down. The only team with more is the Chargers. Sixteen in six games is almost three a game, always on the same down.

Think about what a second-down penalty does. You’ve got second-and-six, a comfortable spot. You can run it, you can use play-action, the whole playbook is open. Then comes a hold, a false start, or an illegal formation, and suddenly it’s second-and-sixteen. From there, you’re most likely ending up in third-and-long. And on third-and-long, as we just saw, we convert one in five.

It’s a chain. The second-down penalty leads to third-and-long. Third-and-long leads to the punt. And the punt leads to one-four-and-one.

On Monday in San Francisco, we saw the whole chain at once: five flags on the offensive line, seven penalties as a team, and three of twelve on third down. That wasn’t bad luck. That was the whole season packed into one game.

The Full Picture From the League

Here’s how the league sees us right now. ESPN has us at twenty-nine. CBS, with Pete Prisco, has us at twenty-eight. Fox has us at twenty-seven. Pro Football Talk has us at thirty, with Florio’s line that Quinn’s time is up.

Not one of them has us higher than twenty-seven, for a team that was playing in the NFC Championship Game nine months ago. That’s a massive drop in how the league sees Washington.

Part Eight: Who’s Really to Blame?

I’m going to close this part with something that might surprise you.

A lot of people are going to look at that third-and-long number and point straight at David Blough. You can’t call four verticals on every third-and-long, and so on. There’s some truth to that.

But I think Keim’s number points somewhere else.

Ten of forty-eight on third-and-long is awful, but almost every team is bad on third-and-long, because it’s a situation where the defense gets to choose what happens. What’s not normal is getting there forty-eight times. And what gets you there is sixteen penalties on second down.

That’s not a play-calling problem. It’s discipline. It’s detail. It’s the same lack of attention to detail we saw on fourth down Monday night, and with the clock against the Giants.

And in the NFL, discipline is on the head coach.

So for me, Keim’s number doesn’t save Quinn, and it doesn’t separate him from Blough. It puts him right in the middle. It’s the stat that best explains why Rapoport’s line, the one about the long-term future still being a question, exists in the first place.

Tell me in the comments: who’s more to blame for those forty-eight third-and-longs, Blough or the sixteen second-down penalties?
