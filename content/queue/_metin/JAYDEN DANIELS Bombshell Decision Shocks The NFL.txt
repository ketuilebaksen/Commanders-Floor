The pressure around Washington has been growing all season, and the conversation keeps coming back to one thing: the decisions being made when games are on the line.

Dan Quinn has now spoken up publicly for his offensive coordinator, David Blough, a young play caller facing serious criticism after several puzzling late-game moments. Quinn’s defense was clear: he believes Blough is talented, competitive, and doing more work behind the scenes than fans can see.

But a lot of people had questions about that defense, especially after the ending against the Rams. Then came Thursday night in New York, when Washington closed out a 24–21 win over the first-place Giants with a late touchdown.

So did that win silence the critics? Or is the late-game problem still very real? Let’s break it all down.

Welcome to Commanders Floor. Hail to the Commanders. Let’s go.

Part One: What Dan Quinn Said

Quinn discussed the issue in an interview with Scott Abraham of 7News in Washington.

When the conversation turned to Blough and the calls that have come under scrutiny, Quinn described him as an incredibly competitive and talented guy. He also stressed that people outside the building don’t see everything that goes into those decisions.

That’s a reasonable point, in one sense. Fans see the call and what happens next, but they don’t know every discussion or consideration that came before it.

Still, in the NFL, the final decision is what everyone remembers.

Washington has struggled to close out games, and the calls being questioned have come at exactly the moments when there’s no time to recover. Quinn wants the blame shared by coaches and players instead of landing on one assistant. But the criticism has been hard to separate from Blough, because his offense and his play calling have been at the center of several of these late-game situations.

Part Two: The Play Everyone Is Still Talking About

The biggest example came against the Rams.

Washington needed a touchdown with only seconds remaining. Instead of calling a pass into the end zone, Blough called for Jayden Daniels to throw underneath to tight end Chig Okonkwo.

Okonkwo broke a couple of tackles, got to around the 10-yard line, tried a lateral, and the clock ran out. No touchdown.

The moment immediately raised a basic question: with the game at stake and almost no time left, why wasn’t the ball going to the end zone?

Jay Gruden’s Blunt Take

That question isn’t just coming from frustrated fans.

Former Washington head coach Jay Gruden gave a blunt assessment on 106.7 The Fan. He called the decision unexplainable and said it simply can’t happen.

Gruden’s point was that NFL teams only get one chance each week to win a game. In his view, Washington had two ways to keep its hopes alive: push the game into overtime, or go for two and try to win it at home against a Rams team that was fighting to save its own season.

His criticism was direct because he sees the call as a failure of basic game management, not just a play that didn’t work. In the final seconds, the play has to match the urgency of the moment. A throw underneath that burns the remaining time leaves no chance for another attempt.

That’s why the decision has become such a big part of the conversation around Blough and Washington’s late-game execution.

Jayden’s Response

Jayden Daniels tried to defend the play afterward, but his response didn’t quiet the concerns. He didn’t offer the kind of emphatic public defense of his coordinator that might have changed the conversation.

Let me be careful here: that doesn’t prove there’s any conflict between Jayden and Blough, and nobody should stretch his comments into one. But it does mean the support for that play wasn’t strong enough to put the criticism to rest.

And remember what Adam Peters said on the radio: the bottom line is that the ball needs to get in the end zone there. He didn’t name anyone, but he didn’t defend the call either.

Part Three: It’s Not Just One Play

The Rams ending isn’t the only moment people have pointed to.

Gruden also brought up Washington’s two-point conversion attempt against Philadelphia in Week 1, when Jayden threw a shovel pass to John Bates, a backup blocking tight end.

Gruden questioned the choice, noting that Bates has very little career production as a receiver. The criticism isn’t that a player in that role can never be targeted. It’s that, in a high-pressure moment, the choice seemed unusual enough to invite scrutiny.

That Week 1 play matters because it shows how long this conversation has been building.

And then there’s the rest of the list we’ve covered on this channel:

The Giants tie: the clock ran out in overtime before Washington could attempt a game-winning field goal. Quinn himself admitted to ESPN that it took entirely too long to execute the strategy.
The 49ers loss: the fourth-down confusion, the viral sideline clip, and Quinn’s explanation about a headset question.
The Rams loss: a delay-of-game penalty on a key fourth down, according to SB Nation, plus the checkdown at the end.

When questionable calls happen in separate games, the reaction becomes bigger than any one play. Fans start asking whether there’s a pattern, whether the staff is learning from its mistakes, and whether the right people are making the calls when the game is tight.

Part Four: Thursday Night Changed the Conversation, a Little

Now here’s where things get interesting, because Thursday night happened.

Washington went into MetLife Stadium on a short week, in cold, rainy conditions, against the first-place Giants, in what everyone called the ultimate must-win game. And Washington won, 24–21.

Late in the fourth quarter, with the Giants leading after Isaiah Likely’s 66-yard touchdown, the offense had to answer. And it did. Jayden found Stefon Diggs, and Diggs turned it into a catch-and-run touchdown that gave Washington the lead for good.

According to NBC Sports, Diggs finished with six catches on ten targets for 83 yards and the game-winning score. Then the defense held, and a J.J. McCarthy mistake sealed it.

So, in the biggest late-game moment of the season so far, Blough’s offense delivered.

That matters. After the Giants tie, the 49ers loss, and the Rams loss, Washington finally closed out a close game in the fourth quarter. And it did it on the road, on a short week, against the division leader.

But Let’s Be Fair

One win doesn’t erase the pattern.

Jayden threw two poorly timed interceptions on Thursday night. The Giants controlled much of the first half, outgaining Washington and owning time of possession. And the offense still had stretches where it couldn’t move the ball.

So Thursday was a step forward, not a cure. If Washington wants to prove the late-game problems are behind it, it has to keep doing it, week after week.

Part Five: The Head Coach’s Responsibility

The bigger issue goes beyond the offensive coordinator.

Dan Quinn is the head coach, and the ultimate responsibility for preparation and decisions rests with him. He’s said he wants better execution from both coaches and players. But game management is part of his job.

The head coach has to make sure the team understands what each situation demands and is prepared to respond with discipline. When the same kind of end-of-game trouble keeps showing up, Quinn can’t avoid questions about the system around his play caller.

That doesn’t mean every failed late-game decision belongs to Quinn alone, or that players have no responsibility. Football is a team sport, and execution matters.

But the head coach sets expectations, assigns responsibilities, and has the authority to step in. If a young coordinator is having repeated problems in crucial moments, it’s fair to ask whether Quinn should be more involved in those moments and provide clearer direction.

And remember the idea we pitched on this channel: give Jayden Daniels more control at the end of games. Let the franchise quarterback read it and decide. That’s one way to ease the pressure on a young play caller.

Part Six: The Player Most Affected

There’s another concern in the middle of all this: Jayden Daniels.

He’s the franchise quarterback, and he’s been part of multiple late-game situations that drew criticism. That doesn’t mean he’s the cause of every problem. It shows how much is being asked of a young quarterback when the people around him may not have enough shared experience to make those high-pressure moments feel settled.

We talked earlier this week about how passive Jayden has looked: 70 percent of his throws against the Rams were behind the line of scrimmage or within nine yards. Some of that comes from pressure. Some comes from the play calling. And some comes from a receiver room that keeps changing because of injuries.

When the quarterback isn’t confident in the plan, it shows up most at the end of games.

Part Seven: The Coaching Turnover Nobody Talks About

Here’s some context that explains a lot.

Chris Russell of ESPN 980 pointed to several departures from Washington’s coaching and support staff last offseason:

Cliff Kingsbury, the former offensive coordinator, now on the Rams’ staff.
Brian Johnson.
Tavita Pritchard.
Bobby Johnson, the offensive line coach.
Dave Gardi, the vice president in charge of game management.

Russell also noted that several defensive assistants left, and that Washington brought in a few replacements from outside the organization.

His argument is that the amount of turnover helps explain why the team has struggled in so many areas.

The Game Management Departure Stands Out

Look at that list again: the vice president of game management left.

Think about what that means. The person whose job was specifically to help with clock management, timeouts, challenges, and end-of-game situations is gone. And this season, Washington has had repeated problems with clock management, timeouts, and end-of-game situations.

That could be a coincidence. But it’s worth noticing.

Context, Not an Excuse

That observation adds context, but it doesn’t erase the current staff’s responsibility. Every team adapts when people leave, and Washington still has to find answers with the coaches and players it has.

Experience matters, especially when you’re trying to handle the details that decide close games. But the practical question for Washington is what Quinn and his staff do next, not just who was or wasn’t there last season.

Part Eight: The Blough Question

Let’s talk directly about David Blough.

Blough is 31, and this is his first season as an NFL play caller. He was expected to bring something new to the offense and build on what Kingsbury had done.

Instead, the current system has raised questions about whether Jayden is being asked to process too much, and whether the scheme fits his strengths. Remember the numbers Grant Paulsen pointed out a few weeks ago: Washington is in the shotgun 66 percent of the time and under center only 27 percent, ranking 30th in the league. Under center is where real play-action lives, the kind that gets linebackers to step up and opens the middle of the field.

Those are concerns, not final conclusions. But they’re part of the conversation, because the offense hasn’t made its late-game decisions look clear or dependable.

The First-Year Shield

Blough’s inexperience might explain why the scrutiny is so intense. But it can’t be a permanent shield. The job comes with responsibility, and his calls will be judged by their timing and their consequences.

At the same time, Quinn chose this staff, and he’s responsible for helping a first-time coordinator succeed. If Quinn believes Blough is talented and competitive, the next step is making sure that talent is backed by a process that produces sound decisions under pressure.

And to be fair to Blough, Thursday night was a good sign. In a must-win situation, with the game slipping away, his offense drove for the go-ahead touchdown. That’s exactly what critics have been demanding.

Part Nine: The Defense Has Questions Too

Quinn’s challenge isn’t only on offense.

Daronte Jones, Washington’s 48-year-old first-time defensive coordinator, faced criticism after a Washington starter complained that the defense failed to adjust to the same plays against the Rams.

Think about Puka Nacua’s fourteen catches for 150 yards in that game. When one receiver beats you that many times, the question is why nothing changed.

And on Thursday night, Isaiah Likely went over 100 receiving yards with a 66-yard touchdown. A tight end gashing your defense is another sign that adjustments are still a work in progress.

The source of the problem may be different from the offensive issues, but the bigger concern is the same. Can Washington recognize what’s happening, make the needed adjustments, and give its players a clear plan?

That’s part of why Steve Wilks has been moved to the sideline on game days, to help Jones with exactly those in-game adjustments.

Part Ten: The Path Forward

So what does Quinn do now?

Defending a coach’s character and work ethic is one thing. Fixing the decision-making process is another.

Fans want evidence that Washington can manage the clock, choose the right calls, and give Jayden and the offense a realistic chance when a game is hanging in the balance.

Here’s what I want to see:

One: a clear end-of-game plan. Every scenario practiced and decided ahead of time. Nine seconds at the 31? Everyone already knows the call before the situation happens.

Two: more involvement from Quinn in critical moments. He’s the head coach. He has the authority, and the responsibility, to step in.

Three: more trust in Jayden. He protected the football almost all season before Thursday. Let him own more of the end-of-game decisions.

Four: in-game adjustments on defense. If a receiver or tight end is beating you over and over, change something. With Wilks on the sideline, there’s no excuse not to.

Five: build on Thursday. The go-ahead drive in New York was exactly what this team needed. Now do it again.

Part Eleven: Why This Matters Now

The timing could not be more important.

After Thursday’s win, Washington is three-five-and-one, two games behind the five-three-and-one Giants. The season is alive again.

Next up is Monday night, November 23rd, against the Cincinnati Bengals.

If Washington wants to make a real run at this division, it has to win close games. And close games are decided in exactly the moments this conversation is about: the final drive, the final timeout, the final play call.

Thursday showed it’s possible. Now the staff has to prove it wasn’t a one-time thing.

The Bigger Picture for Quinn and Blough

Remember what we’ve covered all month.

Ian Rapoport reported that Josh Harris doesn’t like firing coaches midseason, but that some wins have to come.

Adam Peters went on the radio and said the ball needs to get in the end zone.

Pro Football Talk wrote that the case for changing the people running the game from the sideline had grown stronger.

And then Washington won in New York.

That win doesn’t make the criticism disappear. But it changes the tone. Quinn bought himself time, and Blough had his best late-game moment of the season. If they keep stacking wins like that, the conversation shifts from “who should be fired” to “can this team catch the Giants?”
