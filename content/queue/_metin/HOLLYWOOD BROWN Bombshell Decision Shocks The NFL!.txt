Hollywood Brown is on the open market. A first-round pick with 4.27 speed, available for almost nothing. And Washington’s receiver room is begging for exactly what he does best: stretch the field.

Washington had first dibs on him over teams like the Steelers and Vikings during the waiver process, and it didn’t use them. Brown cleared waivers on Monday. Now he’s a free agent, and any team can sign him.

So the question is simple: will Adam Peters make the call now? And what does it tell us if he doesn’t?

Plus, Antonio Williams played 86 percent of the snaps against the Giants and got only two targets. I’ll tell you why he keeps disappearing from the game plan. And ESPN gives Washington a 13.9 percent chance to win the division. I think they got it wrong.

Welcome to Commanders Floor. Hail to the Commanders. Let’s go.

Part One: How Hollywood Brown Hit the Market

Last Thursday night, Jordan Schultz reported that the Eagles were parting ways with Marquise “Hollywood” Brown. On Friday, the Eagles made it official with a statement on their website: they had waived Brown.

According to the Philadelphia Inquirer, citing a source who spoke to ESPN, Brown was released at his own request, with a financial settlement that gives the Eagles some relief.

When Brown was asked whether he expected to play more in Philadelphia, he said yes, he did, but that he understands things happen.

That’s a player who didn’t fight with anybody or make a scene. He simply asked out of a team that’s two-and-seven with seven straight losses.

And according to Schultz, Brown is expected to land with a playoff contender.

The Waiver Rule Everyone Got Wrong

There was some confusion about this, so let’s clear it up.

I’d read in some places that Brown, as an eight-year veteran, would go straight to free agency without passing through waivers. That’s wrong. The Eagles’ own website made it clear: after the November 10th trade deadline, every released player, veterans included, is subject to waivers through the end of the playoffs.

Why did that matter so much? Because the waiver order isn’t decided by who calls first. It’s decided by record. The worst teams get first dibs.

The two teams generating the most buzz for Brown were the Steelers, who lost to Cincinnati on Sunday and dropped to six-and-three, and the Vikings, who are six-and-two. Washington, at three-five-and-one, was ahead of both of them in the waiver order.

It was one of the few times losing games actually gives you an edge.

Washington Didn’t Use It

According to Mike Garafolo, Brown cleared waivers on Monday and is now free to sign with any team at any time.

That means Washington, along with everyone else ahead of the Steelers and Vikings, passed on claiming him.

To be fair, there’s a logic to that. If you think Brown will choose a contender anyway, or you’d rather negotiate a new deal than inherit his current one, you might pass on the claim and try to sign him as a free agent instead.

But now Washington has lost its advantage. It’s on equal footing with every other team, and the contenders, including Pittsburgh, Minnesota, Kansas City, and San Francisco, are all in the conversation.

My stance doesn’t change: claimed or signed, Brown needs to be in Ashburn before Monday night’s game against the Bengals.

Part Two: Is Hollywood Brown Still Worth It?

Let’s be fair, because I’m not going to sell you a Pro Bowl season he didn’t have.

With the Eagles this year, Brown played in four of nine games, with one start, 120 snaps, eight targets, five catches, and 63 yards. In late September, he suffered a grade-one ankle sprain.

Five catches in four games. That’s the number everybody who says he isn’t worth it will point to.

But Look at the Rest

On SI flagged a detail I love: all five of Brown’s catches this season went for first downs. All five. Every time they threw it to him and he caught it, he moved the chains.

Last season, in Kansas City, he finished with 49 catches, 587 yards, and five touchdowns.

His best season was 2021 in Baltimore, with 91 catches and more than 1,000 yards.

For his career, he has 376 catches, 4,385 yards, and 33 touchdowns.

He’s 29. He was the 25th overall pick in 2019. And he ran the 40-yard dash in 4.27 seconds at his pro day.

4.27. Even if he’s lost a step, he’s still faster than any receiver Washington has right now.

Part Three: Washington’s Receiver Room Needs Him

Now think about Washington’s roster.

Terry McLaurin is out for the season with a torn patellar tendon.

Dyami Brown, the other receiver who could go over the top, has been on injured reserve since November 7th.

What’s left:

Stefon Diggs, who plays mostly in the slot.
Treylon Burks, who, according to Pro Football Reference, has an average depth of target of 17.8 yards, but who keeps going in and out of the training room.
Antonio Williams, the rookie.
Van Jefferson.
Luke McCaffrey.

That’s the group Jayden Daniels has to take into a game against a Bengals offense that’s been putting up big numbers.

Max Dible of Heavy wrote that Brown is a receiver with straight-line speed who can serve as a legitimate deep threat, and that a phone call and an offer to Brown’s agent make plenty of sense.

I’ll go one step further. It’s not just sensible. It’s mandatory.

Why Speed Matters Even When It Doesn’t Catch the Ball

A defense isn’t scared of a receiver because of what he’s done. It’s scared of what he can do on the next snap.

If there’s a 4.27 guy lined up outside, the safety can’t come down into the box. He can’t help on Diggs over the middle. He can’t guess.

Even if Brown catches only two balls a game, those two balls force the other team to change its whole coverage.

That’s exactly what Washington lost with Terry, and Brown can give it back, at least in part.

And remember what we covered last week: Jayden threw 70 percent of his passes behind the line of scrimmage or within nine yards against the Rams. One reason is that defenses have been sitting back in two-high looks, knowing Washington has no consistent deep threat. Brown changes that math.

Part Four: The Money Is the Best Part

Now the money, and this is the best part.

According to Pro Football Talk, the bulk of Brown’s one-year deal with Philadelphia was a signing bonus, which the Eagles already paid. The base salary was small.

As a free agent, Brown would sign a new deal with Washington, and it would almost certainly be cheap: a prorated veteran deal for the rest of the season.

And Washington, according to Spotrac back in October, was one of the teams with the most cap space in the entire league.

So it doesn’t cost a draft pick. It doesn’t cost meaningful cap space. And it doesn’t cost anything down the road. His deal ends in March. If it doesn’t work out, you release him, and that’s that.

It’s the smallest risk Peters can take all season.

The Eagles’ Brutal Detail

Here’s a detail that’s almost funny.

According to On SI, by signing Brown in the offseason, the Eagles lost a 2027 sixth-round compensatory pick they would have received for losing Reed Blankenship to Houston. And they don’t get it back by releasing him.

So the Eagles essentially paid a draft pick to have Brown for four games. Sorry, Philly, but that made me laugh a little.

Part Five: The Other Side

Now let’s be fair, because there are real arguments against.

First: the Eagles didn’t use him. They have the worst offense in the league in yards per game, according to the Inquirer, and it wasn’t because they were loaded at receiver. DeVonta Smith and other receivers missed big chunks of the season, and Philly ended up relying on young receivers. Even in that situation, Brown played only 21 percent of the snaps, according to the Eagles’ official site. They saw something in practice that we didn’t.

Second: the ankle. He’s coming off an ankle injury that bothered him in September. And at 29, speed is the first thing to go.

Third: learning a playbook in a week. It isn’t easy to join a new team midseason. Against the Bengals, he’d probably have a small package of routes at most.

Fourth, and this is the uncomfortable one: every snap Brown plays is a snap somebody else doesn’t get. If he comes in, somebody loses a role.

All of that is real, and I’m putting it on the table.

The Real Question

But here’s what matters most.

Jordan Schultz said Brown is expected to land with a playoff contender. So the real question is: does Washington consider itself a playoff contender or not?

If Peters believes this team can fight for the division, and in a minute I’ll show you why I think it can, then signing Brown is a five-minute decision.

If he doesn’t sign him, and lets him go to Pittsburgh, Minnesota, Kansas City, or San Francisco, Peters is telling us with his actions what he won’t say with his words: that he doesn’t really believe in this season.

Remember, at the November 10th deadline, Washington stood pat because the front office said it believed it was still in the race. If that’s true, letting a free deep threat walk would contradict that. That’s two signals in one week, and they point in opposite directions.

Look, I could be wrong. Maybe Brown shows up, catches three balls in eight games, and I have to come back here and explain why I was screaming for a guy the Eagles didn’t want. It could happen.

But the cost is close to zero, and the ceiling is a 4.27 receiver lined up next to Jayden Daniels. I’ll make that bet every time.

Part Six: The Antonio Williams Problem

And if Brown does arrive, there’s a rookie in that locker room whose role is already ridiculously small. And he’s the guy who’s on the field more than anyone.

According to Athlon’s snap counts, Antonio Williams played 59 of 69 offensive snaps against the Giants. That’s 86 percent. No Washington receiver played more.

Treylon Burks played 42 snaps. Van Jefferson played 24. Luke McCaffrey played 14.

And how many targets did Williams get? Two.

Two, on a night when Jayden threw 33 passes.

And with those two targets, Williams had 42 yards and a 33-yard touchdown on third-and-1. Every time they threw to him, something good happened. And they still didn’t go back to him.

Riggo’s Rag Says It Out Loud

Ryan McCafferty of Riggo’s Rag wrote a column with a headline that gets right to the point: Washington has to stop treating Antonio Williams like a secondary option.

Citing CBS Sports, he points out that Williams is second on the team behind Diggs in catches (28), targets (41), and yards (326). But he hasn’t caught more than five passes in a single game this season. And Thursday’s touchdown was only his second of the year, and his first since Week 1.

The snap trend is what jumps out at me most. According to Riggo’s Rag, in the first three weeks, Williams was playing less than half the snaps. Then it climbed into the high 60s. Over the last three games, it’s been in the 80s.

So the staff trusts him to be on the field. They play him more than anybody. But when it’s time to throw the ball, he disappears from the plan.

McCafferty wants him getting at least seven or eight targets per game, and he closes with a great line: it’s time to make him the present.

Part Seven: My Tactical Read

Here’s my tactical read, and fair warning, it’s mine.

When a receiver plays 86 percent of the snaps and gets two targets, there are two possible explanations.

Explanation one: he’s out there to block and clear space for others. His job is to run the route that pulls the safety, so Diggs comes open over the middle. That happens, and it’s part of the game.

Explanation two: the passing game is built around one player. Against the Giants, Diggs got ten targets out of 33. Almost a third.

Don’t get me wrong: Diggs scored the game-winner, and he earned it. But an offense that depends on a receiver who’s almost 33 winning one-on-one on every third-and-5 is an easy offense to read.

Cincinnati’s defensive coordinator is going to watch that film and think the same thing I’m thinking: if I take away Diggs, who beats me?

The answer has to be Antonio Williams.

My Number

So I’ll say it plainly: against the Bengals, Williams has to get eight targets. Not two, not four. Eight.

If that means Diggs gets six instead of ten, so be it. Not because Diggs doesn’t deserve the ball, but because an offense that spreads it around can’t be shut down with one coverage.

How Brown and Williams Fit Together

This ties directly into Hollywood Brown.

If Peters brings in Brown, it isn’t so he can take targets from Williams. It’s the opposite.

Brown stretches the field outside. The safety stays deep. And Williams’s intermediate routes open up.

Brown opens the field, and Williams eats in the middle. They don’t compete. They complement each other.

And most importantly: Williams is the only receiver in this group who’ll still be here in 2027 and 2028 on a rookie deal. He was a third-round pick, 71st overall. Every target he gets now is an investment. Every target he doesn’t get is a missed chance to find out whether Washington has a starter for Jayden’s window.

The Red Flags on Williams

I owe you the other side here too.

First: if he’s never caught more than five passes in a game, that could mean he isn’t separating enough to earn more. Targets aren’t handed out. If he’s not open, the quarterback doesn’t throw it to him.

Second: he’s a rookie. Sometimes a staff protects a young player, gives him a simple role, and lets him loose gradually. David Blough might have a plan we don’t see.

Third: Jayden threw two interceptions in the fourth quarter against the Giants. When a quarterback is coming off that, he looks for the receiver he trusts most, and right now that’s Diggs.

But I keep coming back to the same thing: you don’t give 86 percent of the snaps to a receiver you don’t trust. If he’s on the field, let him play. If he’s playing, throw him the ball.

Maybe Blough reads this, throws fourteen passes to Williams against the Bengals, and the kid drops two. I’ll live with that. But two targets in 59 snaps isn’t a plan. It’s a waste.

Part Eight: The NFC East Just Got More Open

All of this, Brown, Williams, the targets, matters for one reason. On Sunday, something happened that left this division more open than it was on Friday.

The Cowboys lost 46–40 to the 49ers. They gave up 46 points and dropped to four-and-six.

Here’s the NFC East now:

Giants: five-three-and-one.
Cowboys: four-and-six.
Commanders: three-five-and-one.
Eagles: two-and-seven, coming off their bye with seven straight losses.

So Washington is basically even with Dallas. The Cowboys have one more win, but also one more loss.

And the Giants are two games ahead, with a detail that’s worth gold: against the Giants, Washington is one-and-oh-and-one. If the teams end up tied, the tiebreaker belongs to Washington.

ESPN’s Number

According to Bryan Manning of Commanders Wire, ESPN’s Seth Walder puts Washington in the sixth tier of his scale, the one he calls “Stranger Things Have Happened.”

The numbers:

14 percent to make the playoffs.
13.9 percent to win the NFC East.
0.3 percent to make the Super Bowl.

13.9 percent is roughly a one-in-seven chance. I don’t buy it, and it isn’t just because I’m a fan.

Look at the Schedule

Here’s what’s left:

Monday: Cincinnati, at home.
Then: at Arizona.
Then: at Tennessee.
Then: Houston, at home.
Then: Atlanta, at home.
Then: at Minnesota.
Then: at Jacksonville.
Week 18: Dallas, at Northwest Stadium.

Yes, Cincinnati is tough. But look at the middle of that schedule: Arizona, Tennessee, Atlanta. That’s not the Bengals.

And one host on 106.7 The Fan said he has a feeling the Week 18 game against Dallas at Northwest Stadium will be a win-or-go-home game.

I’ll go further: that game could be the division final.

The Math

Here’s the math, and it’s mine.

If Washington wins six of its last eight games, it finishes nine-seven-and-one.

To stay ahead of Washington, the Giants need a better record, because in a tie, the tiebreaker goes to Washington. So the Giants would have to win at least five of their last eight games.

Can they? Of course. But the Giants we saw on Thursday, the team that let Washington beat them with a touchdown in the final minute, don’t look like a team that safely wins five of eight.

And Dallas, which just gave up 46 points, doesn’t either.

So 13.9 percent? I think it’s higher than that. And that’s exactly why signing Hollywood Brown makes sense. If you believe there’s a real path to this division, you add the cheap deep threat sitting on the open market.

Final Word From Commanders Floor

Let me put it all together.

Hollywood Brown asked out of Philadelphia and was released in what was reportedly a mutual decision. Washington had priority over teams like the Steelers and Vikings on waivers, but didn’t claim him. He cleared waivers on Monday and is now a free agent.

The case for signing him: 4.27 speed, 4,385 career yards and 33 touchdowns, all five of his catches this season went for first downs, he’d cost almost nothing, and Washington badly needs a deep threat with Terry McLaurin and Dyami Brown out.

The case against: the Eagles barely used him, he’s coming off an ankle injury, and learning a new offense midseason is hard.

My verdict: sign him before Monday night. If Washington really believes it’s in the race, this is a five-minute decision.

Antonio Williams played 86 percent of the snaps against the Giants and got two targets. He needs at least eight against the Bengals, and Brown would make his life easier, not harder.

And the NFC East: Dallas lost 46–40 to San Francisco. Washington is two games behind the Giants with the tiebreaker in hand, and ESPN’s 13.9 percent feels too low.
