Three days. That’s all that stands between Dan Quinn and what could be Josh Harris’s most drastic decision yet.

Ian Rapoport already put it out there: some wins have to come. And Harris’s ownership group has fired a coach in the middle of a season before, in another sport.

Meanwhile, the trade deadline is today at 4 p.m. Eastern. The Washington Post’s headline says Washington should neither buy nor sell. A new report says the Patriots could be the team to watch for Stefon Diggs. And to me, “stand pat” is the worst advice of the week.

Welcome to Commanders Floor. Hail to the Commanders. Let’s go.

Part One: Where Things Stand

Let’s start with the facts.

Washington is two-five-and-one. According to Athlon, the Giants are five-two-and-one. That’s a three-game gap.

If Washington loses Thursday night at MetLife, the gap becomes four games with eight to play. In a division that won’t produce a wild card, that’s basically the end of the season.

The Same Mistake, Three Times

After Sunday’s 20–13 loss to the Rams, Dan Quinn opened his press conference by calling it a frustrating game and saying, once again, that the team hasn’t nailed its complementary football, and that he’ll keep saying it until it does.

That “I’ll keep saying it” is exactly the problem, because he’s said it a lot.

According to SB Nation, on a key fourth down in that game, the play call ended in a delay of game. Jayden Daniels took the blame for it.

And according to Athlon, that’s the third time this season a late-game or fourth-down sequence has gone wrong:

The overtime tie with the Giants, when the clock ran out.
The loss to the 49ers, with the fourth-down confusion and the viral sideline clip.
And now the Rams, with the delay of game and the checkdown with nine seconds left.

After the Giants tie, Quinn himself admitted to ESPN that it took entirely too long to execute the team’s strategy.

Three times, the same kind of mistake. And an owner has been watching.

Part Two: What Was Said in October

In October, four messages all pointed in the same direction. We already broke them down on Commanders Floor, so let me move fast.

Tom Pelissero: Harris has never believed in midseason coaching changes.

Mike Garafolo: Harris would love for Quinn to stay.

Ian Rapoport, October 21st: Josh Harris doesn’t like firing a coach midseason, and some wins do have to come. And on the 27th, he named Quinn as the other coach to watch on the hot seat.

The Line Nobody Went Back and Read

Then there’s the one almost nobody read all the way through.

On October 19th, John Keim of ESPN reported that a source close to leadership told him Quinn’s job was not in immediate jeopardy and wasn’t likely to change during the season.

But Keim added a detail that feels huge today. He wrote that an ugly loss in San Francisco likely wouldn’t change anything.

Look at that: San Francisco. Keim drew the line of what was tolerable at one specific game. That game already happened, and Washington lost it.

Then came the 27–10 win over Philly, and then the loss to the Rams. Since the “some wins have to come” warning, Washington is one-and-one.

Part Three: What Harris’s Group Has Already Done

This is the part that makes me think things could move faster than everybody’s saying.

Everybody keeps repeating that Harris doesn’t fire coaches midseason. With the Commanders, that’s true. He fired Ron Rivera on January 8th, 2024, after the season was already over.

But Harris doesn’t just own the Commanders. His group, Harris Blitzer Sports and Entertainment, also owns the New Jersey Devils in the NHL.

And in March of 2024, in the middle of a season that was falling apart, the Devils fired their head coach, Lindy Ruff, and named an interim coach.

Is hockey the same as football? No. Did Harris make that call personally, or was it his hockey people? I don’t know, and I’m not going to make it up.

But when everybody says Harris never does that, I’m telling you his organization already has. When a team falls apart and the plan isn’t working, the Harris group knows how to pull the plug midseason.

Part Four: The Four Scenarios, With My Odds

These numbers are mine, not from any insider. They’re my read of everything I just showed you.

Scenario one: Quinn finishes the season, and Harris decides in January.

That’s what everything reported points to. Harris doesn’t like changes midstream, the locker room backs Quinn according to Keim, and the injuries explain part of the struggles.

I give it 40 percent that Quinn finishes the season and is let go in January.

Scenario two: fired before the season ends.

If Washington loses Thursday and falls four games back with eight to play, there’s nothing left to save in the standings, and the conversation changes.

I give it 20 percent right now. If Washington loses Thursday, I’d say it climbs to about a one-in-three chance.

Scenario three: the partial firing.

Harris doesn’t touch Quinn, but somebody on the staff goes, or some game-day decisions get taken away from Quinn. It’s the drastic decision that doesn’t get called a firing.

I give it 15 percent.

And remember the version of this I pitched yesterday: hand Jayden more control at the end of games, so the head coach isn’t the one managing those final sequences. That would fall into this category.

Scenario four: Quinn stays for 2027.

For that, Washington needs a strong finish, winning most of the eight games after Thursday, and the story becoming “when this team is healthy, it competes.”

I give it 25 percent.

Adding It Up

Add scenarios one and two together, and by my read there’s about a 60 percent chance Dan Quinn isn’t Washington’s head coach next year, and about a 20 percent chance he doesn’t even finish this season.

Part Five: My Take. And It’s the Hardest One.

I’m warning you, this one is tough.

If Washington loses Thursday at MetLife, Josh Harris has to fire Dan Quinn on Friday. Not in January. Friday.

I know that sounds brutal for a coach who took this team to a conference championship game. Let me explain why.

First: the mistake keeps repeating. That’s not bad luck. It’s the clock against the Giants. It’s fourth down against the 49ers. It’s the delay of game and the checkdown against the Rams. It’s the complementary football that gets talked about every week and never shows up. When the same kind of mistake happens three times in eight games, it’s a process problem, and the process belongs to the head coach.

Second: Jayden Daniels. Every week Jayden plays in a system that isn’t working is a week of his prime he never gets back. We talked yesterday about how passive he’s become: 70 percent of his throws against the Rams went no more than nine yards downfield. Eight games under an interim coach would also give Harris something useful: a look at how Jayden responds to a different voice before he picks the next head coach.

Third: the message. If you lose Thursday and do nothing, you’re telling the locker room and this city that being four games back with eight to play is acceptable. It’s not.

What You’ll Say Back

I know the arguments against, and they’re fair.

“Interim coaches never work.” Almost always true. But you don’t fire him to save this season. You fire him to start the search before everybody else does.

“The locker room is with Quinn.” Keim reported it, and I believe it. That says a lot of good things about Quinn as a person. But locker-room support doesn’t convert a fourth down.

“There are a ton of injuries.” There are: Terry McLaurin, Daron Payne, Javon Kinlaw, Laremy Tunsil. But no injury calls a play that ends in a delay of game on fourth down.

Look, I could be wrong. Everything that’s been reported says Harris waits until January. And if Washington wins on Thursday, I’ll lower my own numbers. But if the standings say four games back on Friday morning, don’t tell me nobody warned you.

Tell me in the comments: if Washington loses Thursday, does Harris fire Quinn on Friday? Yes or no?

Part Six: Deadline Day

While Harris is looking at his head coach, his general manager is on an even shorter clock: today, 4 p.m. Eastern.

And there’s some very serious advice floating around about what Adam Peters should do.

The Washington Post ran a piece with a headline that’s making the rounds all over the DMV. It asks whether Washington should buy or sell at the deadline, and its answer is “neither.”

I’ll tell you up front, I couldn’t read the full article, so I’m not going to pretend I know all of the writer’s arguments. I’m going to argue with the idea in the headline, because that’s what’s going to stick in everybody’s head.

And that idea isn’t alone.

Adam Peters, on 106.7 The Fan before the Rams game, said the results of the game weren’t going to dictate what Washington does.

Dean Jones of Riggo’s Rag wrote on Sunday that the loss makes buying harder to justify, and that Washington needs to either sell or stand pat.

Part Seven: The Patriots and Stefon Diggs

And there’s a new name in the Stefon Diggs story.

Matt Burke of Complex says that if Washington sells, Diggs will have a market, and the most likely candidate is the New England Patriots. His reasoning: Diggs and New England parted on good terms.

That’s Burke’s read, not a report of any negotiation. I want to be clear about that.

The context: Diggs is 32, on a one-year deal. According to Complex, he has 27 catches, 252 yards, and five touchdowns this season. No other Washington receiver has more touchdowns. And in March, he’s a free agent.

Burke also wrote a line that sums up Washington’s season better than anything: the only real path to the playoffs at this point is winning a mediocre NFC East. And in that NFC East, the Giants are three games up.

Part Eight: Why “Stand Pat” Is the Worst Option

Here’s what nobody is saying.

“Don’t buy, don’t sell” sounds smart. It sounds like a serious general manager, someone who doesn’t make emotional decisions after a loss.

But look at what it means in practice.

It means taking Diggs into Thursday, win or lose, and then watching him walk for nothing in March.

It means getting nothing back for the contracts that expire after this season: Diggs, Chaisson, Dyami Brown, and the rest.

And it means that if Thursday goes wrong, Washington wakes up on Friday four games back, with the deadline already closed and not one extra pick for the rebuild.

Standing pat is a decision too. And it’s the only one of the three that guarantees you get nothing.

My Verdict on the Deadline

Standing pat is the worst of the three options. It’s worse than buying wrong and worse than selling cheap.

Yesterday, I said I’d sell Diggs for a third-round pick or better, and keep him for anything less, because of the message it sends before Thursday. I’ve thought about it more, and I’m adjusting my line, right here, in the open.

I’m selling Diggs today, to the Patriots or to whoever pays a reasonable pick.

I don’t care that he’s the team’s touchdown leader. A 32-year-old receiver on a one-year deal, on a two-five-and-one team, is exactly the kind of player a smart team sells in November.

What do I ask for? I don’t have a reported number on the Diggs market, so I’m not going to make one up. But my rule is simple: any real pick is worth more than what Diggs gives Washington after March, which is nothing. A third would be great. A fourth or fifth is still a win. I’d only hang up on a seventh-round throwaway.

Selling Diggs Isn’t Giving Up on Jayden

And this is the part that’s going to cost me in the comments.

Selling Diggs isn’t giving up on Jayden. It’s the opposite.

Every pick Washington stacks today is a piece to put around Jayden in 2027, which is the year that matters. And remember the 2027 draft: it’s deep, and it’s being held right here on the National Mall.

Plus, think about what we talked about yesterday. Jayden’s receiver room is already unstable. Only two receivers in the Rams game had caught a pass from him earlier this season. The answer isn’t keeping a 32-year-old who leaves in March. The answer is giving those snaps to Antonio Williams and Treylon Burks, who will be here, and adding a young receiver who can grow with Jayden.

Buy what lasts. Sell what expires.

That’s not either option in the Post’s headline. It’s both at the same time.

Sell: Diggs for any real pick. K’Lavon Chaisson for a fourth or better. Dyami Brown for a Day 3 pick.

Buy: a young receiver who stays, like Josh Downs, whom Ian Rapoport says Washington could get for a third or fourth, or Brian Thomas Jr. if Jacksonville finally deals.

Don’t buy: half-season rentals. No Tony Pollard-type moves at two-five-and-one.

What I Think Peters Will Do

My gut says Peters is going to do something closer to what the Post’s headline suggests than what I’m asking for. He said the results wouldn’t dictate his moves, and he’s been reluctant to sell all season.

I hope I’m wrong. But if 4 p.m. comes and goes and Washington does nothing, Peters will own that decision just as much as if he’d made a bad trade.

Part Nine: Thursday Night Ties It All Together

Thursday night at MetLife connects every storyline.

For Dan Quinn: win, and the countdown pauses. Lose, and by my read, there’s a real chance Friday is his last day.

For Jayden Daniels: he called it a must-win. He needs to throw downfield, trust what he sees, and make the hero play he didn’t make against the Rams.

For Josh Harris: it’s the evidence. A rough patch, or a pattern?

For Adam Peters: whatever he does by 4 p.m. today, Thursday will show whether it was the right call.

And for the Giants? They’re coming off a win, and they lead the division at five-two-and-one. They’ll be ready.
