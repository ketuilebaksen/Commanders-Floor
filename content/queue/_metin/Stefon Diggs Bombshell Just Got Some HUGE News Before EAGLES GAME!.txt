The whole DMV says Stefon Diggs is Washington’s best receiver. Adam Peters just said something different.

He didn’t say it with a cut or a trade. He said it with a name: Antonio Williams. In his words, the sky’s the limit. And the PFF numbers back him up.

But there’s one stat from London nobody wants to look at, and I’m going to show it to you.

Plus, everybody’s going to tell you to just run the ball on Philly. I’m telling you our dead-last third-down offense doesn’t actually start on third down. And Daron Payne just lost a potential buyer, because Chicago went and got Jeffery Simmons.

Welcome to Commanders Floor. Hail to the Commanders. Let’s go.

Part One: What Adam Peters Said

This morning on 106.7 The Fan, Adam Peters talked about Antonio Williams, the third-round rookie receiver. According to Hogs Haven, Peters said that every single day, Williams does something special in practice that shows what kind of talent he has, and that the sky’s the limit for him.

When he was asked whether Williams can carry more of the load now that Terry McLaurin is done for the year, Peters said he’s definitely capable of it.

And this isn’t coming out of nowhere. On Monday, Dan Quinn had already hinted at it. According to the team’s website, Quinn said Williams is one of the players you’d see getting more targets going forward.

So the general manager and the head coach are saying the same thing in the same week. That’s not an accident. That’s a message.

Part Two: The Numbers

Praise from a general manager is worth what it’s worth. So I went to the numbers, because numbers don’t have to be nice to anybody.

These are PFF numbers, via Zach Selby at Commanders.com.

Williams has a 75.2 receiving grade, one of the best among all rookies in the league.

When the ball goes his way, the quarterback has a 96.7 passer rating.

He’s tied for first in catch rate among rookie receivers with at least twenty targets.

He’s third on the team in yards and catches, and with McLaurin out, he moves up that list.

And here’s the number I like most. On comebacks, in-routes, and posts, quarterbacks have a 118.8 passer rating when they target him.

Those are grown-man receiver routes. Routes where you have to win with your body, over the middle or down the field. They aren’t screens or gimmicks. They’re the routes a real number-one receiver runs.

The Versatility Detail

One more detail, a tactical one, and I love it.

Williams has played 74 snaps in the slot and 75 outside. That’s basically even.

You know what that means? The defense can’t know where he’s going to line up. You can move him before the snap to hunt the slowest linebacker or the weakest corner on the field. That kind of versatility is exactly what makes a receiver a WR1.

Part Three: What Nobody Is Saying About Diggs

Here’s the part nobody wants to say out loud, because it’s uncomfortable.

Stefon Diggs leads the team in receiving touchdowns, with four, according to Commanders.com. He has the name, the history, and a career’s worth of highlights.

But Diggs is a free agent in March, and he’s turning 33. And on Wednesday, he didn’t practice because of hand and hamstring issues.

Williams has three more years on his rookie contract.

So the real question isn’t who’s better today, at 8:20 on Sunday night. The question is who we’re playing for over the rest of this year.

At one-four-and-one, for me, the answer is obvious. We’re playing to find out who Jayden Daniels is going to be throwing to in 2027.

And Jayden already knows. Back in Week 1, after Williams’s debut against these same Eagles, Jayden went out of his way to praise a big catch Williams made. You can’t buy that kind of chemistry at the deadline.

Part Four: What Worries Me About Williams

So you don’t think I’m only selling you the pretty stuff, here’s what worries me.

Williams still hasn’t had a big game when the quarterback wasn’t Jayden.

Look at London. With a rookie quarterback throwing to him while Jayden was out, Williams got his biggest workload of the season: eight targets. He finished with three catches for 21 yards. He was targeted four times on third down, and none of those converted. One ball thrown his way was intercepted.

He did have a short catch that he turned into an 87-yard run to the end zone, but it was wiped out by an illegal shift penalty on our own team.

What does that tell me? That the kid depends a lot on his quarterback seeing him in time. Jayden sees him.

So I’m saying this now, before the game, so it doesn’t sound like an excuse afterward: if Jayden’s elbow acts up on Sunday and Marcus Mariota has to come in, my verdict on the targets changes.

The Rookie Wall

The other worry is the rookie wall.

Rookies tend to dip in November, when they have more games on their bodies than ever before. And if Williams becomes the first name on every opposing defense’s scouting report, we’re going to find out what he’s made of.

I think he holds up. But it’s a bet, not a sure thing.

Part Five: The Best Case for Diggs

Now I’ll give you the other side, because on this channel we argue for real.

The best case for Diggs is the red zone. He has four touchdowns, the team lead. When the field shrinks, there’s no room to separate with speed. What wins is craft: using your body, timing, knowing exactly when to throttle down a route. Diggs has more than ten years of that. Williams has six games.

And there’s a second argument: Vic Fangio. On the other side of the ball Sunday is a defensive coordinator who makes a living disguising coverages. He’ll show you two deep safeties before the snap, and once the ball is snapped, one comes down and the other rotates. A rookie can misread that rotation and break his route the wrong way. A veteran has seen it a hundred times.

I accept both of those arguments. That’s why I’m not saying Diggs shouldn’t play. I’m saying he can’t be first in line.

Part Six: How I’d Use Williams Against Fangio

This is the beautiful part. Williams’s best numbers line up exactly with what Fangio’s defense gives you.

A defense with two deep safeties hands you the middle of the field, the space between the linebackers and the safeties. And where does Williams have that 118.8 passer rating? Comebacks, in-routes, and posts. The in-routes attack exactly that middle.

If I’m David Blough, here’s what I’m calling:

On first and second down: put Williams in the slot and run him on an in-route at twelve yards, right behind the linebackers. Pair it with play-action so the linebackers take one step forward. That opens the window behind them, and that’s where he lives.

On third and long: send him outside against the corner on a comeback. That’s a throw Jayden can complete in his sleep.

And for Diggs: use him in the red zone, where his craft matters most. Use him on key third-and-shorts, where you need a guy who knows how to get open in a phone booth.

Part Seven: My Verdict

Williams has to be the WR1 on Sunday. Maybe not on the depth chart, but in targets.

And I’m putting a number on it so you can hold me to it: if Williams gets fewer than seven targets against Philly, Blough is getting it wrong.

I’ll go even further, and I know you’re going to come at me in the comments for this. Diggs should be the third receiver in targets. Not because he’s done, but because every ball that goes to a guy who leaves in March is a ball that doesn’t go to a guy who’s staying.

Tell me in the comments: who should be the WR1 on Sunday, Williams or Diggs?

Part Eight: The Number That Made Me Close the Laptop

But careful. You can have the best rookie receiver in the league, and if you can’t convert third down, none of it matters. And on third down, Washington is the worst team in football.

The team’s website put out its preview numbers, and one of them made me close the laptop and go for a walk.

Third-down offense: 30.86 percent. Thirty-second. Dead last out of 32 teams.

Seven out of every ten times we reach third down, we don’t convert.

The rest of the offense is middle of the pack:

19th in total yards, at 325 a game.
20th in points, at 22 a game.
22nd in passing, at 200 yards a game.

There’s one thing that works: the run game, ninth in the league at 125.2 yards a game.

And there’s one more thing in our favor: Washington is second in the league in turnover differential, at plus-six. This team protects the ball and takes it away.

Part Nine: The Philly Numbers

Now look at the opponent.

According to Commanders Wire, Philadelphia’s defense is dead last in the NFL in EPA per rush.

EPA stands for expected points added. It measures how many points each play adds to, or takes away from, an offense’s expected score. Being last means that, every time you run on Philly, you get closer to scoring than you would against any other defense in the league.

According to Commanders.com, the Eagles give up 131.4 rushing yards a game, 27th in the league.

Their offense, with Tanner McKee at quarterback because Jalen Hurts is out, ranks 31st in total yards and 29th in points, at 18.1 a game. They’re 31st in time of possession, holding the ball 27 minutes and 40 seconds a game. Washington holds it 30 minutes and 21 seconds.

But don’t get comfortable with Philly’s defense on the scoreboard. In points allowed, the Eagles rank 21st, at 25 a game. Washington ranks 31st, at 28.7.

In other words, on paper, Philly defends better than we do. Keep that in mind.

Part Ten: What Nobody Is Saying About Third Down

Everybody is going to say the obvious thing: run the ball. And yes, you should run the ball.

But here’s what nobody is saying. The third-down problem doesn’t get fixed on third down. It gets fixed before third down.

Think about it like this. If you run well on first and second down, you end up in third-and-two or third-and-three. Those are the downs where you can run again, use play-action, throw a quick slant, or let Jayden use his legs. The whole playbook is open.

Now remember the numbers John Keim found a couple of weeks ago. Washington had 81 third downs at that point, and 48 of them came with seven or more yards to go. Washington converted only ten of those 48. On short and medium third downs, the conversion rate was much more respectable.

So the problem isn’t really third down. It’s how often we get into third-and-long.

What puts us there? Second-down penalties. Washington had sixteen offensive penalties on second down through six games, more than any team except the Chargers. A hold, a false start, an illegal formation, and suddenly second-and-six becomes second-and-sixteen. From there, you’re almost always facing third-and-long.

And, as we just saw in London, an illegal shift can wipe out an 87-yard touchdown.

So here’s my key for Sunday: win first and second down.

Run the ball against the worst EPA run defense in the league. Use play-action off that run game to hit Williams on in-routes behind the linebackers. And, above everything else, no pre-snap penalties. Stay ahead of the chains, and the worst third-down offense in the NFL suddenly faces third-and-three instead of third-and-twelve.

That’s how you fix it. Not with a magic third-down play, but by never letting it get to third-and-long in the first place.

Part Eleven: The Payne Update

One more story before we close, because it affects the trade deadline.

Daron Payne is questionable for Sunday with a foot injury. And at the same time, he just lost a potential buyer.

ESPN confirmed that Chicago traded for Jeffery Simmons, sending Tennessee two first-round picks and a fourth-round pick. Remember, Chicago was one of the landing spots Bleacher Report’s Christopher Knox named for Payne a couple of weeks ago, and Bears On SI called Payne a no-brainer for Chicago. Well, the Bears just filled that need with a much bigger name. That door is closed.

So who’s left? Reports have linked the Giants to Payne. Jacksonville still needs interior help, which keeps the Payne-for-Brian Thomas Jr. idea alive. Other contenders may still be shopping. But the list of buyers is getting shorter.

And now there’s a foot injury. If Payne can’t play Sunday, he loses his final showcase before the deadline, and any buyer will want a close look at that foot before giving up a pick.

That’s why I keep saying it: the price only drops from here. Whatever offer Washington can get, a late third or an early fourth, or ideally BTJ straight up, the time to take it is now.

Final Word From Commanders Floor

Let me put it all together.

Antonio Williams has the general manager’s endorsement and the numbers to back it up: a 75.2 PFF receiving grade, a 96.7 passer rating when targeted, a 118.8 rating on comebacks, in-routes, and posts, and the versatility to line up anywhere. He’s under contract for three more years.

Stefon Diggs still has the red-zone craft and the experience against Fangio’s disguises. But he’s a free agent in March, he’s turning 33, and he missed Wednesday’s practice. He should play, but he shouldn’t be first in line.

My verdict: Williams gets at least seven targets on Sunday, or Blough is getting it wrong.

The real key: Washington’s third-down offense is dead last, but the fix starts on first and second down. Run the ball against the league’s worst EPA run defense, stay ahead of the chains, and stop the pre-snap penalties.
