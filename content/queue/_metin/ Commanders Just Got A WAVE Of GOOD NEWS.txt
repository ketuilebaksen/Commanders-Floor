Adam Peters just pointed at the sideline. And now, some people are wondering if he should be the one on the chopping block.

Thirty hours before kickoff against the Giants, Washington’s general manager went on the radio and said the ball needs to get in the end zone on that last play against the Rams. He didn’t defend his head coach. He didn’t defend his offensive coordinator.

Back in March, Josh Harris told us exactly how he makes these decisions, and I’ll show you why those words hit twice as hard today. On top of that, Peters’s silent trade deadline is looking worse by the day, and Nick Allegretti is expected to start at center tonight against the Giants.

Welcome to Commanders Floor. Hail to the Commanders. Let’s go.

Part One: What Adam Peters Said

Let’s start with the actual news, because everything else flows from it.

On Wednesday morning, Adam Peters went on 106.7 The Fan, and he was asked about the final play against the Rams.

Quick refresher: Washington was down 20–13, with the ball at the Rams’ 31-yard line and only seconds left. Jayden Daniels threw it short over the middle to Chig Okonkwo. Okonkwo broke a couple of tackles, got to around the 10, tried a lateral, and that was the game.

According to Pro Football Talk, Peters said there were a couple of things on that play, but the bottom line is that the ball needs to get in the end zone there.

According to Heavy, when he was asked whether the play was designed to include a lateral, Peters shut the door. He said he didn’t want to get into the specifics of the play.

And Heavy summed it up this way: Peters didn’t call out anyone by name, but he didn’t defend Dan Quinn or David Blough either. Not one word.

Pro Football Talk read that interview and wrote that the case for changing the people running the game from the sideline is even stronger heading into Week 10.

So the general manager pointed at the sideline, and the league got the message.

Part Two: The Question Nobody Wants to Ask

When I heard that, I asked myself one question.

If somebody in Ashburn has to answer for this season, why is everyone looking only at the head coach? Why is nobody looking at the man who built the roster?

So today, I’m asking the question nobody in the DMV wants to ask out loud: does Josh Harris need to take a hard look at Adam Peters?

And I’m telling you up front, so nobody accuses me of making things up: no insider has reported that Peters’s job is in danger. This is my opinion, built on public information. But I think it’s a conversation that has to happen.

Part Three: Why That Quote Bothers Me

Put yourself in the shoes of a general manager who trusts his head coach.

Somebody asks you about a controversial play the day before a huge game. The textbook answer is: “I trust Dan. We went over everything internally. Our focus is on the Giants.” That answer protects your locker room on a short week.

Peters didn’t say that. He said what a fan would say in the stands: the ball needs to get in the end zone. And he said it thirty hours before a game Quinn himself called a must-win.

Look, I could be wrong. But I see an alibi.

Washington is two-five-and-one. Commanders Wire has already written that Quinn will likely be moved on from after the season. Somebody is going to pay for this season, and there are really two candidates: Quinn and Peters.

If you’re Peters, you go on the radio, you separate yourself from the sideline’s decisions without naming anybody, and then, on the day Harris has to choose, the record shows that the general manager already called out the problem.

If I’m Dan Quinn and I hear that on the radio on my drive to Ashburn, I turn it off and just stare at the steering wheel for a long time.

Part Four: Opening the Ledger

The problem with that alibi is that the record says other things too. So let’s open the ledger.

The Good

Peters arrived in Washington in January 2024.

His first season: twelve-and-five and a trip to the NFC Championship Game. That was real, and nobody can take it away from him.

And drafting Jayden Daniels was the best decision this franchise has made in twenty years. Full stop.

The Bad

Then came 2025: five-and-twelve. And 2026 so far: two-five-and-one.

I did the math. In the Peters era, Washington is nineteen wins, twenty-two losses, and a tie in the regular season.

And since the start of 2025, it’s seven wins, seventeen losses, and a tie. Seven wins in twenty-five games.

That isn’t bad luck. That’s a trend.

The Big Moves

Now look at the big moves, and watch for the pattern.

Laremy Tunsil: Peters traded for him from Houston and paid with draft picks, including second- and fourth-round selections. Then, according to Pro Football Network, Tunsil tore his triceps in training camp this year. Washington paid premium picks for a tackle in his thirties, and he got hurt before the season even started.

The center position: Peters released Tyler Biadasz in February to go after Tyler Linderbaum. Linderbaum signed in Las Vegas. The result: Washington is on its third center, and tonight it’s expected to be Nick Allegretti, who had played only two snaps at center since Week 3.

Deebo Samuel and Marshon Lattimore: more picks sent out of Ashburn to bring in veterans.

The result: today, Washington has its own seven picks in 2027, and not one extra.

Odafe Oweh: according to Heavy, Washington committed close to a hundred million dollars to him in free agency in March. He might turn out to be a great signing. I’m not ruling that out. But it’s another big bet with the same general manager’s signature on it.

The Silent Deadline

And then there’s Tuesday’s trade deadline.

Josh Downs, the young receiver who asked out of Indianapolis and whom Washington was linked to all week, went to Kansas City for a fourth and a fifth.

Keenan Allen went to San Francisco for a sixth-round pick.

Brian Thomas Jr. was taken off the trade block in Jacksonville.

With Terry McLaurin out for the year, Washington brought in nobody. According to reports, Peters even admitted the team tried to buy and came up empty.

And Washington didn’t sell either. Stefon Diggs, K’Lavon Chaisson, Dorance Armstrong, and the other expiring veterans all stayed. Reports suggested several of them could have fetched Day 3 picks.

Putting It All Together

Put it all together: picks handed out for veterans who got hurt or didn’t produce, a center position cut loose with no Plan B, a roster with no replacement receivers, and a silent trade deadline.

Is that on the sideline? Or is it on the man who built the roster?

Part Five: What Josh Harris Said in March

Here’s what I think decides this whole conversation.

On March 30th, at the annual owners meeting after the five-and-twelve season, Josh Harris was asked about Dan Quinn and Adam Peters.

According to John Keim of ESPN, Harris said that Dan and Adam have his confidence.

According to JP Finlay of NBC4, he said something even more important: generally, he’s consulted, but generally, he defers.

And according to Nicki Jhabvala of The Athletic, Harris said he thought Adam and Dan had a great free agency.

Think about what those three quotes mean together.

Harris doesn’t build the roster. He hands it off. He said it himself: he defers.

So every pick traded, every player cut, every signing, and every silent deadline is a Peters decision, made with Harris’s blessing and the confidence he gave in March.

And he gave that confidence to both of them together: Dan and Adam. Not just Dan.

If they were a package deal in March, you can’t fire one in November and act like the other had nothing to do with it.

Harris Is Not Sentimental

And Harris isn’t a sentimental owner.

In October, Keim described him as an owner who separates emotion from his decision-making. And as we’ve covered on this channel, Harris’s group, which also owns the New Jersey Devils, has made midseason coaching changes in hockey, including firing Lindy Ruff in March 2024.

Keim also reported that ownership never gave Quinn a specific mandate before the season. No explicit goal. In owner language, that’s leaving the door open to decide once the results are in.

And back in October, a national columnist said bluntly that there’s a lot of hubris in a front office that hasn’t accomplished anything. Notice that he didn’t say “coaching staff.” He said “front office.” He was talking about Peters.

Part Six: The Case for Keeping Peters

Now let me give you the full other side, because it’s a serious one.

One: Peters brought in Jayden. That alone buys him a lot of credit. If you fire the general manager who picked your franchise quarterback, the next GM will want to build his own system around a player he didn’t choose.

Two: the NFC Championship season happened. It wasn’t a mirage. It was twelve-and-five with a rookie quarterback.

Three: 2025 had huge injury problems. According to Pro Football Network, Jayden played only seven games last season. Without your franchise quarterback, any general manager looks bad.

Four: firing a general manager after three years resets everything. New GM, new head coach, new system, while the window of Jayden’s rookie contract keeps closing as you start from scratch.

All of that is true. And that’s why I understand the people who’ll write in and say, “You’re crazy. Peters needs more time.”

My Answer

I’ll answer with one question.

If the argument for saving Peters is Jayden Daniels, then what did Peters do this year to protect Jayden Daniels?

He didn’t secure the center position. Washington is now on its third center.

He didn’t bring Jayden a receiver when Terry McLaurin went down for the season. Downs went to Kansas City for a fourth and a fifth.

He didn’t bring Jayden a running back when two of them went on injured reserve.

And on Wednesday, he went on the radio and pointed at the sideline.

Jayden isn’t the reason to protect Peters. Jayden might be the reason to ask harder questions about him.

Part Seven: The Scenarios

So what could Harris actually do? Here’s how I see it, and again, these are my scenarios, not reports.

Scenario one: keep both, evaluate in January. That’s what Harris’s history suggests. Both finish the season, and Harris decides after Week 18. This is the most likely outcome.

Scenario two: fire Quinn, keep Peters. That’s what most people expect. Peters gets to hire his second head coach and keeps building around Jayden. Commanders Wire already suggested Quinn will likely be moved on from.

Scenario three: change both. A full reset. New general manager, new head coach. That would be drastic, but if Harris views them as a package, which is how he described them in March, it’s on the table.

Scenario four: keep Peters, but limit his control. For example, Harris could bring in a more experienced football executive above or alongside Peters. It wouldn’t be a firing, but it would be a clear message.

My Take

Here’s where I land.

I don’t think Peters should be fired right now. Jayden Daniels is too important, and the NFC Championship season is too recent.

But Peters should not get a blank check to hire the next head coach without accountability. If Quinn goes after the season, Harris should look just as hard at the roster-building decisions as at the coaching decisions. The Tunsil trade. The center situation. The lack of depth. The silent deadline.

If Harris holds the head coach accountable and gives the general manager a free pass, he’s only fixing half the problem.

Tell me in the comments: should Josh Harris hold Adam Peters accountable too? Yes or no?

Part Eight: Tonight’s Concern. The Center Spot.

Let’s bring this back to tonight, because the roster-building questions show up right on the field.

Lucas Patrick entered the concussion protocol after Sunday’s game. When Patrick went out against the Rams, Matt Goldman stepped in and played 35 snaps. Dan Quinn said both Goldman and Nick Allegretti were options if Patrick couldn’t go Thursday.

Now the expectation is that Allegretti starts at center tonight.

Allegretti has played center before, but he moved back to guard. And according to the reports, he’d played only two snaps at center since Week 3.

And remember why center matters so much. The center doesn’t just snap the ball. He makes the protection calls, reads the front, identifies blitzers, and gets everybody on the same page. Against a Giants defense that likes to bring pressure, that’s a huge job for a player who has barely played the position this season.

This is exactly what I mean about roster-building. A team with Jayden Daniels at quarterback should never be down to its third center in November, with someone who’s played two snaps at the position since September.

Part Nine: What Washington Needs Tonight

Let’s make it simple.

One: protect Jayden. With Allegretti at center, keep the protection calls clean. Use quick throws early. Help Brandon Coleman on the edge with chips and tight ends.

Two: get the ball in the end zone. Peters said it himself. Washington scored three points in the final 44 minutes against the Rams. That can’t happen tonight.

Three: take some shots. Jayden was 70 percent passive against the Rams. If Treylon Burks is back from his foot injury, use him deep.

Four: finish the game. No more end-of-game breakdowns. The clock, the fourth down, the delay of game, the nine-second checkdown: none of it can happen again.

Five: win the turnover battle. That’s how Washington beat Philly, and it’s how it beats the Giants.

Part Ten: Why Tonight Matters for Everyone

Tonight’s game at MetLife ties every storyline together.

For Dan Quinn: a win buys time. A loss puts Washington four games back with eight to play, and the “countdown” we’ve been talking about gets very real.

For Adam Peters: he stood pat at the deadline because he believes in this roster. Tonight is the first test of that belief. And after his radio comments, if the sideline fails again, the scrutiny will land on Quinn. But if the roster fails, it lands on him.

For Josh Harris: tonight is evidence. A rough patch, or a pattern?

For Jayden Daniels: he called it a must-win. Franchise quarterbacks own these nights.
