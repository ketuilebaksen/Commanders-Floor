This isn’t the first time this season we’ve walked away from a weekend saying Jayden Daniels played below his standard.

He isn’t getting much help. The offensive line is struggling, the running game is inconsistent, and some of the play calling has been questionable. But he also isn’t playing at his best.

So what exactly did we see from Jayden on Sunday against the Rams? And is there one number that explains what’s going on?

There is. And it might surprise you.

Welcome to Commanders Floor. Hail to the Commanders. Let’s go.

Part One: Why This Conversation Matters

Jayden Daniels is the centerpiece of this franchise. He’s the reason Washington reached the NFC Championship Game. He’s the reason a new stadium and a new era feel exciting. He’s the guy Josh Harris will eventually have to pay.

But as this team keeps losing, and as his play keeps falling below his own standard, Jayden has become more and more polarizing.

That raises some uncomfortable questions. If you keep holding him to a standard and he keeps falling short of it, does the standard need to change? And what exactly is Jayden Daniels at this point in his NFL career?

That’s what we’re exploring today. I’m going to credit Jayden for what he does well, and I’m also going to take a fair, honest look at what he isn’t doing right now: the areas where he can improve and help this team win more games.

Let me say this clearly up front, because I’ll probably need to repeat it: I’m not blaming this loss on Jayden Daniels. I don’t think he’s playing badly. And I absolutely want him to be Washington’s franchise quarterback for a long time.

But on this channel, we don’t play favorites, not even with our quarterback.

Part Two: Everything Around Him Was a Problem

Before we get to Jayden, let’s be fair about what happened around him on Sunday.

The offensive line: Brandon Coleman, especially in the second half, just wasn’t helping his quarterback. Myles Garrett took over the game after halftime.

The running game: Washington was begging for even forty rushing yards. Rachaad White led the team with 28 yards. You don’t win NFL games like that.

The drops: Stefon Diggs and Antonio Williams both had drops.

The red zone: the defense set the offense up inside the 30 after Nick Cross’s interception, and Washington couldn’t punch in a touchdown. This team keeps struggling to convert in key situations, especially in the red zone.

All of that is true. But it doesn’t mean Jayden didn’t play a part in how this game finished.

Part Three: The Biggest Play of the Game

In my opinion, the biggest play of the game was that fourth-and-17.

Jayden had a chance to convert. Van Jefferson had beaten Emmanuel Forbes’s press coverage on the left side. And Jayden hesitated. He waited. And by the time he threw it, he underthrew it. Turnover on downs.

Those are winning-time moments, to borrow a Dan Quinn phrase. And in those moments, the quarterback wasn’t showing up either.

I want to be fair: it would have taken a very good throw. But franchise quarterbacks make those throws. That was Jayden’s chance to rise above everything else that went wrong, and he didn’t.

Part Four: The Stat That Explains Everything

Here’s where this gets interesting.

For the last two or three weeks, I’ve been saying on this channel that I don’t think Jayden is playing outright badly, but I don’t think he’s playing like Jayden Daniels either. So I went looking for a number to explain it.

I went to Pro Football Focus and looked at target depth, meaning how far downfield he’s throwing the football.

PFF breaks target depth into four categories:

Behind the line of scrimmage
Short: 0 to 9 yards downfield
Medium: 10 to 19 yards
Deep: 20-plus yards

I combined the first two, behind the line and short, into one category that I’m calling “passive throws.” These are throws that don’t take much risk in terms of how far the ball travels: screens, checkdowns, and anything up to nine yards.

Here’s the number: 70 percent of Jayden’s throws against the Rams were passive.

Seven out of every ten passes went no more than nine yards downfield, or behind the line of scrimmage.

That’s the eighth-highest rate of any single game in his NFL career. It’s not number one. But look at the context.

The Three-Game Stretch

The 49ers game ranked as the fifth-most passive game of Jayden’s career.

So I looked at three-game stretches. His last three games, against the 49ers, the Eagles, and the Rams, averaged a 70 percent passive rate. That’s the third-most passive three-game stretch of his career.

The only two stretches that were more passive:

The first three games of his NFL career: 72 percent, when he was a rookie learning the league.
Weeks 9 through 11 of his rookie year: also 72 percent, when he was playing through a rib injury.

Let me boil it down: these last three games, Jayden has been about as passive as he’s ever been in the NFL. The only times he was more passive, he was either a brand-new rookie or playing hurt.

Part Five: Not All Short Throws Are Bad

I want to be very clear about this, because it matters.

I’m not saying every one of those short throws was a bad decision. Some of them were him beating the blitz. Some were hot reads. Some were the quick game doing exactly what it’s supposed to do.

And I have to own something here. Before the Rams game, I said the quick game, five- to seven-yard throws to Antonio Williams and Stefon Diggs over the middle, was the right way to attack the Rams’ zone and two-high safety looks. I still believe that was the right starting point.

But the quick game is supposed to be a weapon, not the whole offense. You use it to set up the shots. You hit short throws until the defense creeps up, and then you go over the top. That’s the part that never really happened on Sunday.

After watching the All-22, it does look like Jayden is simply not taking some of the deeper shots that are there.

Part Six: Why Is Jayden Playing This Way?

This is the important part. Why is this happening? I think there are several factors, and most of them aren’t only on Jayden.

Factor one: the play calling.

Could Jayden not be fully in sync with the play calls? It’s possible. If the game plan leans heavily on short, quick throws, and the play caller doesn’t build in shot plays, the quarterback will be passive by design. That’s on the coaches as much as the player.

Factor two: the offensive line has conditioned him.

Has the pressure this line keeps allowing made Jayden a little skittish, a little jumpy? Yes, probably. Absolutely.

When you get hit over and over, you start feeling ghosts. You start getting the ball out early even when you don’t have to. You stop trusting the pocket long enough for a deep route to develop. That’s a natural human reaction, and it’s what happens to quarterbacks behind struggling lines.

Factor three: an unstable receiver room.

This one blew my mind. In the Rams game, only two receivers had caught a pass from Jayden earlier this season: Stefon Diggs and Antonio Williams.

None of the others, including Luke McCaffrey, Van Jefferson, and Jaden Bradley, had caught a pass from Jayden this year before Sunday.

Think about that. Terry McLaurin is gone for the season. The receivers around Jayden keep changing. Timing between a quarterback and his receivers is built over hundreds of reps, and Jayden is throwing to players he’s barely worked with in games.

That’s not a stable environment. And in an unstable environment, a quarterback is going to default to the safest throws.

Part Seven: The Confidence Question

When you put it all together, here’s what I see: right now, Jayden just doesn’t look like he has much confidence in himself, or in the offense around him.

That shows up in very specific ways.

One: taking his drop too deep. If he doesn’t trust the offensive line, he might drop back deeper to buy time. But going too deep actually creates better rush angles for the edge rushers and lets them get home faster. It’s a vicious cycle: the line struggles, he drops deeper, the rush gets easier, and the line looks even worse.

Two: not throwing to the open receiver in front of him. Sometimes the throw is there, and he doesn’t pull the trigger.

Three: hesitating. That fourth-and-17 to Van Jefferson is the perfect example. He waited a beat too long, and the window shrank.

The Eagles Game Example

Think back to the Eagles game. Remember Jayden’s slow start, especially in the first half?

Remember how he was late on three or four quick out-routes to the sideline? That’s because he didn’t trust it. He was waiting, hesitating.

And then, when he did throw, he knew he was late, so he tried to overcompensate by throwing the ball too far ahead of the receiver, and it sailed out of bounds.

That’s what a quarterback without confidence looks like. Not bad, not inaccurate by nature, just a split second behind, and then trying to make up for it.

Part Eight: What Jayden Does Well

Now let me give him credit, because there’s plenty.

He protects the football. In the Rams game, he threw no interceptions. The fumble came on a strange play where Myles Garrett batted the ball out of his hand. For a young quarterback in a struggling offense, protecting the ball the way he has is a real skill.

He’s still productive. Against the Rams, he went 24 of 35 for 274 yards and a touchdown. Against the 49ers, he threw for 300 yards and two touchdowns. Those aren’t the numbers of a quarterback who’s lost.

He’s keeping this team in games. Washington has been in almost every game this season. A big reason is that Jayden isn’t throwing games away.

He’s still a weapon with his legs. Even when the line breaks down, he can escape and extend plays.

So this isn’t a broken quarterback. It’s a talented quarterback playing scared of mistakes, in an offense that isn’t giving him a reason to be aggressive.

Part Nine: How Washington Fixes This

So what’s the fix? It’s not just “Jayden, throw it deeper.” It has to come from everybody.

From the coaches: build in the shot plays.

David Blough has to design plays that give Jayden clean, defined deep shots, especially off play-action. Remember the numbers we covered a few weeks ago: Washington is in the shotgun 66 percent of the time, sixth-highest in the league, and under center only 27 percent of the time, 30th. Under center is where real play-action lives, the play-action that gets linebackers stepping forward and opens up the deep middle.

Remember the best play of the Eagles game? Treylon Burks’s 54-yard catch on a classic shot concept, two go routes with a post over the middle. That’s the kind of call that gives a quarterback confidence. Call it more.

From the offensive line: give him one clean pocket.

It doesn’t have to be perfect. But Brandon Coleman needs help against elite rushers, whether from chips, tight ends, or slide protection. If Jayden can trust the pocket for two and a half seconds, those deep throws come back.

From the receivers: catch the ball.

Drops kill confidence. When Diggs and Williams drop catchable passes, the quarterback starts doubting the throw before he makes it.

From the front office: stabilize the receiver room.

This is where the trade deadline comes in, and it’s today at 4 p.m. Eastern. Jayden is throwing to players he’s barely worked with. Adding a young, talented receiver who can grow with him, like Josh Downs, whom Ian Rapoport says Washington could land for a third- or fourth-round pick, or Brian Thomas Jr., who already caught seventeen touchdowns from Jayden at LSU, would directly address the instability we just talked about.

And from Jayden: trust what you see.

When the throw is there, let it go. When the receiver wins, give him a chance. The quarterback who reached the NFC Championship Game as a rookie took those shots. That quarterback is still in there.

Part Ten: Does the Standard Need to Drop?

Let’s go back to the question from the beginning: if Jayden keeps falling below his standard, does the standard need to change?

My answer is no.

Jayden’s standard was set by what he did as a rookie: an NFC Championship Game run, big throws in big moments, and fearless play. That standard isn’t a fantasy. He’s already reached it.

What’s changed isn’t his talent. It’s his environment: a broken offensive line, a lost number-one receiver, an unstable receiver room, a play caller who leans on the quick game, and a running game that can’t keep defenses honest.

Put him back in a stable environment, and I believe the standard comes back.

But he also has to meet the environment halfway. Even in a bad situation, franchise quarterbacks find a way to make one or two hero plays a game. That fourth-and-17 was one of them.

Part Eleven: Thursday Night Is His Chance

And the chance to prove it comes right away.

Thursday night, at MetLife Stadium, against the Giants, on a short week. Jayden himself called it a must-win after the Rams game.

The Giants lead the division at five-two-and-one. Washington is three games back at two-five-and-one. A loss basically ends the division race. A win keeps it alive.

Here’s what I want to see from Jayden on Thursday:

One: at least three or four real downfield shots. Not just checkdowns.

Two: trust the pocket when it’s clean. Don’t drop too deep.

Three: throw on time. No hesitating on the outs.

Four: make the hero play when it comes. Because it will come.

If he does those things, his passive rate drops, and Washington’s chances go way up.

Final Word From Commanders Floor

Let me put it all together.

Jayden Daniels isn’t playing badly, but he isn’t playing like Jayden Daniels either.

The stat: 70 percent of his throws against the Rams were passive, behind the line of scrimmage or within nine yards. Over his last three games, he’s been about as passive as at any point in his career. The only times he was more passive, he was a brand-new rookie or playing hurt.

The reasons: the play calling, an offensive line that has conditioned him to get rid of the ball early, and an unstable receiver room where only two receivers had caught a pass from him this season before Sunday.

The symptoms: dropping too deep, hesitating, not pulling the trigger, and overcompensating when he’s late.

The fix: shot plays from the coaches, protection from the line, catches from the receivers, a young receiver from the front office, and trust from Jayden himself.

And the standard stays where it is, because he’s already shown us what he’s capable of.
